# The M4 codebook

## What this table is

This folder holds the data table `m4-codebook.csv`, which since 22 October 2026 is the blind-recoded table described below under “The recoding of 22 October 2026”; the table it replaced, kept for the record as `m4-codebook-illustrative-2026-10-22.csv`; and this explanation. The table has one row for each of a set of enactments drawn from Barak Medina’s account of how the Israeli Supreme Court polices the Knesset’s power to make constitutional rules. Medina is a professor of law at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and his account is the article that the conjecture note at the root of this repository examines (Medina, 2026). The Knesset is Israel’s parliament, with 120 members. The Court, when it reviews the acts of public bodies, sits as the High Court of Justice.

Most rows are Basic Laws (the statutes that together serve Israel in place of a single written constitution) or amendments to them that were challenged before the Court. Three rows are different. The first row, Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty of 1992, is in the table because the Court recognized it as binding in a civil appeal, and that recognition is the benchmark for what a valid constitutional enactment looks like; its row is marked as the control in the `case` column. The election-financing statute of 1969 was an ordinary statute. It had not passed with the sixty-one votes that Article 4 of Basic Law: The Knesset requires for a departure from equal elections, and the Court struck it; Medina stresses that the ground was the equality principle in Article 4 and not the vote count alone (Medina, 2026, p. 693). The enactment behind the 1977 Resler case needed checking. Medina cites Resler, with the signal “see, e.g.”, for the proposition that when the law previously declared invalid was re-enacted with the Article 4 majority the Court refrained from striking it (Medina, 2026, pp. 693–694). Two other sources describe the case differently and agree with one another. The Court’s own account, in its 1981 judgment in Agudat Derekh Eretz, is that Resler held that the Elections (Confirmation of Validity of Laws) Law of 1969 had validly ratified the election laws by absolute majority, and that the 1969 law said nothing about whether those laws met the equality principle. Klinghoffer’s 1983 article, as recorded in the research notes from its abstract page and its sixth footnote (the full article was not read), says the provisions challenged in Resler were in the Elections (Modes of Propaganda) Law of 1959, which governs election propaganda including broadcasting time, and that the Court refused to decide the equality question because the 1969 law had excluded it from review. A third source, Gal Dor’s 2000 article, does not appear to discuss Resler (the search and its limits are in the research log) but confirms that the 1969 law passed by special majority within two days, less than two weeks after Bergman. The row has been recoded on those sources, with the Hebrew judgment still unread, and the sources are listed in the row and in the research log. The `enactment_type` column records which rows are ordinary statutes.

The set is not yet complete even for the article. The 1996 Meatrael case, on an override clause added in 1994 to Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation, has a row whose cells were filled in the recoding (Medina, 2026, pp. 697–698); its outcome cell is flagged, for the reason the recoding section gives. The three cases that followed Bergman on election rules (Medina, 2026, p. 693) have no rows yet.

Each row records how the enactment scores on signs that the Court, as Medina reports it, uses when it decides whether the Knesset abused its constituent power (the Court’s term for the power to make or change constitutional rules, as distinct from ordinary lawmaking). The table is called a codebook because, in social science, a codebook is the document that defines each variable in a dataset and the values it may take. It is called M4 because that is its label in the note’s appendix of matrices.

Three facts about Israel’s system explain why the table is worth keeping. Israel’s constitution is a hybrid of written and unwritten elements, and the written part, the Basic Laws, has been enacted one law at a time since 1958. The Knesset can enact or amend a Basic Law by a simple majority, through the same process it uses for an ordinary statute (Medina, 2026, pp. 683–684), apart from any provision that requires sixty-one votes to amend; the article discusses one, Article 4 of Basic Law: The Knesset, adopted in 1958 and the only such provision among the Basic Laws before 1992 (Medina, 2026, pp. 690, 693). And because the government of the day controls the Knesset in Israel’s parliamentary system, the body that changes the constitutional rules is the body those rules are meant to restrain (Medina, 2026, pp. 684, 690, 699).

## Why it exists

The note makes a claim that only data can test. The claim is that the Court’s doctrine on constitutional amendments is a real decision rule: an amendment’s fate depends on how far it departs from Israel’s unwritten constitution (the principles the Court treats as fundamental, among them free and equal elections, the rule of law, the separation of powers, judicial independence, and the protection of human rights), on how broad the support for it was, and on whether its purpose was to serve the sitting government. The rival claim, which Medina’s article answers and which the note keeps as a live alternative, is that the doctrine is a tool by which a liberal elite protects its position, so that an amendment’s fate depends on its ideological direction. The note’s first test, T1, asks which claim the record supports.

This table is where that record is kept. It holds nine rows: eight drawn from the enactments Medina’s article discusses or cites, and the 1969 ratification law, which the article does not name. On the day the note was written their values were transcribed from the note’s matrix labelled M4 and then partly corrected from the research notes; that version is the illustrative table kept in this folder. On 22 October 2026 every predictor cell was recoded blind under the revised T1 and the outcomes were attached last; that version is the table now named `m4-codebook.csv`. The table is still far from the record T1 needs, because the unit the protocol defines has many more rows than nine. Until it is complete, the claim cannot lose, and the first run said exactly that: it was inconclusive before any outcome was looked at.

## The recoding of 22 October 2026

The note’s revised T1 separates four roles so that no one who codes a predictor has seen how the case came out. On 22 October 2026 the nine rows were recoded that way. Two coders, working separately from a coding sheet that named the enactments by title and date and pointed to the gazette text, the Knesset’s vote tables and the readings, coded every predictor except litigation timing; they were forbidden any judgment, any page about litigation over an enactment, and every file in this repository. An adjudicator settled their eleven disagreements from the same sources under the same rules. A docket reader, working at the same time and apart from the adjudicator, coded litigation timing and then the outcomes, with judgment dates. The sheets, the agreement statistics, the adjudications, the docket and the results are in the research folder for that date, and the code that assembles the table and runs the checks is in the analysis folder.

What the run found is in `research/coding-2026-10-22/results.md`, and the short version is this. The two coders agreed on most cells, and most of their disagreements were one coder finding a record that the other had not. Four rows have no value for coalition-only support, because no permitted source gave a breakdown of the final vote by faction: the 1992 Basic Law, both 1969 statutes, and the 2023 Incapacity amendment. A row with a blank among the four signs, the tier or the direction is excluded from the test as uncoded, so five rows entered the primary analysis. Of those, one was discordant in the note’s sense (the Court’s signs and the enactment’s direction predicting different outcomes): the 2020 budget-deadline amendment, which scores on all four signs and was coded neutral in direction. The protocol requires six such rows before any result can count, and its detectability check, computed from the number of rows and the number of adverse outcomes without looking at which rows were adverse, found that no result on five rows could reach the threshold either way. So the first run is inconclusive under the rule fixed in advance. The one discordant row came out as the decision-rule claim predicted, and that is reported as exploratory and counts for nothing.

Three things the run taught are worth more than its statistic. The first is that the protocol can be executed end to end from legislative records with the Knesset’s own websites unreachable, which they were throughout, for every role. The second is that the Court’s signs and ideological direction do not move together perfectly even in nine rows, since the one row with all four signs is neutral in direction; the fear recorded in the next section, that the test would favor the decision-rule claim by construction, is answered in part by that row. The third is a gap between two ways of reading purpose. The blind coders gave the Incapacity amendment no named beneficiary, because its text names no officeholder, and the illustrative table had scored the prime minister as its beneficiary from the circumstances Medina reports, the pending petition above all (Medina, 2026, p. 701). The protocol tells the coders to read beneficiary from the text and the explanatory notes, so they followed it; the Court read purpose from the circumstances. Whether the protocol should admit circumstantial evidence of a beneficiary was a design question for the next revision, and the note’s change log records that it could not be settled by asking which rule makes this row count. It was settled the same day, before any further coding, by adding a second beneficiary column with a closed evidence rule, described in the column list below, and the primary analysis now uses that column.

Two outcome cells are flagged. The Nation-State law is coded adverse because the Court upheld it and read three of its sections narrowly, and the protocol says that counts as adverse; a reader who does not treat harmonising interpretation as narrowing would code it the other way. The 1994 override clause is coded not adverse by the docket reader, who could not verify from the sources reached whether the Court qualified the override’s shield; Medina reports that it did (Medina, 2026, pp. 697–698), which under the protocol’s rule would make the cell adverse. The cell was left as the docket reader coded it and referred back for a second reading of the judgment, because changing an outcome after assembly on the author’s reading of the article is the move the protocol exists to prevent. Neither flag changes the verdict.

## Why the columns are what they are

Six of the columns record signs of abuse drawn from Medina’s account of the Court. Four of them he gives as the Court’s guidelines, in a sentence of his own that reports them together: an amendment that addresses a specific interest of the current government, takes immediate effect, is supported only by members of the coalition (the parties that form the government and together hold a majority of seats), and is enacted through an expedited process is likely to be classified as an abuse of power unless proven otherwise (Medina, 2026, p. 699). The fifth, how sweeping the amendment’s scope was, Medina attributes to the Court’s reasoning in the Reasonableness case and also uses in his own analysis of the Incapacity case (Medina, 2026, p. 701). The sixth, how the amendment’s timing related to pending litigation, appears only in Medina’s narrative of the Incapacity case (Medina, 2026, pp. 687, 700, 701) and is attributed to the Court nowhere in the article. Elsewhere the article lists whether an amendment takes immediate or delayed effect as a factor in its own right (Medina, 2026, pp. 700, 708). The set of six is this project’s reading of Medina’s account. English translations of four judgments have been read for individual cells, and the research notes cite them: Bergman, Mizrahi Bank, Agudat Derekh Eretz, and the Reasonableness judgment. The six signs have not been checked as a set against any judgment, and that is open work.

The reason for taking the signs from the Court’s practice, as Medina reports it, deserves a plain statement. We did not invent five of the signs; the sixth, timing relative to litigation, is Medina’s observation, and we adopted it. We did choose which descriptors became columns, and we assigned every value, and both of those choices can be criticized. What the choice buys is narrower than fairness. A reader who suspects that the table was built to confirm the note can check each column against the Court’s judgments once they are read. But under the elite-interest claim the Court’s stated reasons are part of the tool, so a test that enters those reasons first and then asks whether ideological direction adds anything favors the decision-rule claim by construction wherever the reasons and direction move together. Direction has now been coded blind for every row, and the two do not move together perfectly even in nine rows: the one row on which all four signs are present, the 2020 budget-deadline amendment, was coded neutral in direction, and two of the three rows coded illiberal, the 1994 override clause and the 2018 Nation-State law, score on two signs only. Before the recoding the rows appeared to move together, if era was taken as a rough proxy: the rows that score on the signs are enactments of the governing coalitions of 2016 to 2023, the 1992 row was bipartisan, and the 1969 financing statute was a cross-party bill. The two budget rows of 2016 and 2020 were coded neutral. The Resler check produced one row of a different shape: the 1969 ratification law was expedited and enacted in response to a judgment, yet it passed by absolute majority (its party breakdown is not known), and the Court declined review. Whether it is discordant cannot be said: its direction is now coded neutral, but breadth of support is exactly what its record does not show, since the recorded tally of 68 to 8 to 5 does not establish which factions voted in favor. Under the revised T1 it is in any case an Article 4 row, reported descriptively rather than put through the prediction table, which was built for the constituent-power doctrine the Court did not hold in 1969. Its own outcome is Not adverse, since the Court held it validly passed; what the Court declined to review was the election laws it ratified, and those are not rows. So the rows in hand that could separate the two claims were expected to be the three that were abusive in form on the illustrative cells, the 2020 budget-deadline amendment and the two 2023 amendments, all with adverse outcomes already known. The recoding found one of them, the 2020 amendment, both abusive in form and neutral in direction, which is what the note’s author expected; the Incapacity amendment lost two of its signs on the enacted text and its coalition-only cell for want of a faction breakdown, and the Reasonableness amendment was coded illiberal, so neither separates the claims. T1 was revised on 22 October 2026 to handle all of this. Under the revision, every predictor except litigation timing is coded from legislative records by two coders who have not read the judgment, this note or this table, litigation timing and the outcomes are added afterwards by a separate docket reader, each claim’s prediction for every row is fixed by a table before any outcome is seen, and only the rows where the two predictions differ are counted, in an exact permutation test with a pre-stated minimum of six such rows and a detectability check run before outcomes are revealed. The primary analysis covers Basic Law rows decided from 1995 onward; the 1969 statutes are reported under the Article 4 rule instead. The revision and its decision rule are in the note under the heading “T1 as revised on 2026-10-22”. The two claims can be stated without the ordering. The elite-interest claim predicts that outcomes track ideological direction and that the Court’s stated reasons track direction too. The decision-rule claim predicts that outcomes track the four form signs, the deviation tier and opposition support, as the note’s prediction table fixes, and that direction has no effect once those are known. They differ only where the two predictions diverge, which the note’s prediction table fixes in advance. The content constraint of the sliding scale enters the test only where a sharp deviation concerns a structural value such as elections and the enactment is neutral in direction; where the deviation concerns identity or rights, a sharp deviation and an illiberal direction are very nearly the same judgment and no row can separate the claims. The inconclusive outcome is defined in advance: fewer than six discordant rows, a failed detectability check before outcomes are revealed, or a permutation p of 0.05 or more. The eight rows coded on the day the note was written were coded before the revision, and all nine rows were recoded under it on 22 October 2026.

The remaining columns describe what the signs are applied to, and the values each may take are these.

- `case` names the enactment, and the case in which the Court dealt with it where that is the usual name.
- `year` is the year of enactment.
- `enactment_type` is Basic Law, Basic Law amendment, or Regular statute, with a note where the enactment was a temporary provision.
- The sixth sign, `litigation_timing`, records whether the enactment was passed while litigation it bears on was pending. An enactment passed in response to a judgment already given is coded as no pending litigation with the response noted, as in the 2016 and 1969 rows.
- `situated_beneficiary` is Yes if, on the day of passage, the enactment’s operative provision would alter the legal position of an identifiable person then in office, or of the sitting government, in a matter then live, and No otherwise. A matter is live only on facts dated no later than the day of passage, from a closed list: a proceeding pending before a court concerning that person or matter; a formal step already taken against them, such as an attorney general’s opinion, an indictment or a petition served; a fixed deadline the enactment moves, where it binds the sitting government or an officeholder; a single-instance or sunset clause, where it applies to the sitting government’s own measure or term. In every item the alteration must be one that successors or other actors would not share, so a clause that touches every incumbent alike satisfies none. Judgments, commentary and belief about motive are excluded, and an enactment whose operative provision is general, with only a motive connecting it to a live matter, is No. The column was added on 22 October 2026 after the first run (the note’s change log item (ac)), because the text-only beneficiary sign fires only where the drafting declares the beneficiary and the Court does not require that. It is coded by a separate reader who has not seen any outcome or judgment, and it is the beneficiary sign the primary analysis uses; `named_beneficiary`, from the text and explanatory notes, is kept and used in a secondary analysis, so that the two can be compared. Its first coding, later the same day, met six of the nine expectations recorded in advance and missed three, all on the list’s temporal items: the two 1969 statutes and the 1994 override clause were coded Yes on their single-instance and sunset clauses. The misses stand as coded. The note’s change log item (ad) records them as a defect in the list as written, and item (ae) tightens the list to the wording above, with fresh expectations recorded before any coding under it; the nine cells coded under the first wording stay in the table, marked with the list version in the provenance column, until a fresh reader recodes them, and the verdict of the first run did not change.
- `scope` is Yes if the enactment removes a check on the government or a protected right, or renders it practically inoperative, in either case without an alternative mechanism, and No otherwise. A one-time exemption from a rule is No. This is the definition adopted on 22 October 2026; it replaces an earlier column, `sweeping_scope`, whose values mixed breadth with duration and had no scoring threshold. Medina uses the word sweeping of the Reasonableness amendment, where he attributes it to the Court, and of the Incapacity amendment, in his own analysis (Medina, 2026, p. 701); both are coded Yes as this project’s judgments under the definition.
- `temporary_provision` is Yes if the enactment was passed as a temporary provision or applies to a single instance, and No otherwise. Medina describes both budget amendments as provisional measures and, in his own assessment of the 2021 Shafir case, treats the provisional nature as evidence of abuse (Medina, 2026, pp. 699–700). It is recorded as a sign, but it is not in the primary form score, which uses only the four signs Medina reports as the Court’s set.
- `targets_judiciary` is Yes if the enactment’s subject is judicial review, judicial appointments or judicial independence, and No otherwise. It exists so that the rows used to test the institutional self-interest rival can be identified from the table. It was coded blind on 22 October 2026 and is Yes for one row, the Reasonableness amendment.
- `direction` is Illiberal if the enactment’s text or stated purpose moves the constitutional order toward preferring Jewish identity over equal citizenship, restricts the rights of minorities or women, or narrows judicial review or judicial independence; Liberal if it moves the other way on any of those; and Neutral if it does none of these, which includes fiscal, procedural and electoral housekeeping measures. Every cell was coded on 22 October 2026 by two coders who had not read the judgments, this repository’s notes, or this table, with their one disagreement settled by an adjudicator under the same restrictions.
- `deviation_tier` records how far the enactment departs from the unwritten constitution, in the steps of the sliding scale that Medina reads in the Court’s approach (under which the greater an amendment’s departure, the broader the support it needs). The values are None, Entrenches, Limited, and Sharp, with the protected value engaged recorded alongside. Entrenches is Medina’s own description of the 1992 Basic Law (Medina, 2026, p. 684). Limited and Sharp follow his distinction between a limited contradiction and a sharp one (Medina, 2026, pp. 684, 702). None is for an enactment that engages none of the five protected values. A first-day label, Near sharp, was used for the Nation-State Basic Law on the strength of Medina’s conjecture that the Court affirmed the limit on the Knesset’s power “likely because” that law came close to the threshold (Medina, 2026, p. 698); it was retired on 22 October 2026, and the cell was then coded blind with no option excluded; the coders gave the Nation-State law Limited. In the illustrative table every tier value other than the 1992 row’s Entrenches, which is cited to the article, was this project’s coding, assigned with knowledge of the outcome the Court reached, which is a defect for T1 because a predictor derived from the outcome cannot test it. In the recoded table every tier value comes from the two coders working from legislative records without the judgments, this note or this table, which does not make them blind to outcomes they already know from public life, and the note says so.
- `outcome` records what the Court did.
- `source` records where each row’s values came from.

In the illustrative table, every value belongs to one of three classes. A value from the article carries no marker in the cell and is cited by page in the row’s `source` column. A value from the research notes, or from a source read in the verification pass, carries the word provisional and names the research item or the source. A value that is this project’s own judgment carries the words “this project’s coding”. A blank cell is a value that has not been found in a source the row can cite. The recoded table records provenance differently: its `provenance` column says for each predictor whether the two coders agreed, the adjudicator settled it, or both left it blank, and the source and supporting quotation for every cell are in the coders’, the adjudicator’s and the docket reader’s files in the research folder for 22 October 2026.

The contested variable, ideological direction, was given its definition first and its values only afterwards, by blind coding, so that its coding can be criticized on its own, apart from the Court’s columns.

## How to read a row

Take the row for the Incapacity amendment. In March 2023 the Knesset amended Basic Law: The Government so that a prime minister could be declared incapable of serving only for medical reasons, and only by large majorities of both the cabinet and the Knesset. At the time, a petition (an application asking the Court to review a public act) was pending that asked the Court to declare Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu incapable because of a conflict of interest, and the amendment passed shortly before the Attorney General (the government’s chief legal adviser, who also represents the state before the Court) was due to respond to that petition (Medina, 2026, p. 687). The research notes recorded a conflict between two reports of that deadline, tracked as issue 5, and the judgment itself resolves it: the response was due on 12 March 2023; on 13 March, at the Attorney General’s request, the Court extended the deadline to 26 March; the amendment passed on 23 March; and on 29 March the Court struck the incapacity petitions. The timing value, litigation pending, stands.

In the illustrative table the row scores the amendment this way. The named beneficiary is the prime minister. It took immediate effect. Only coalition members voted for it. Its scope is coded Yes, as this project’s judgment under the definition adopted on 22 October: the amendment rendered the incapacity provision practically inoperative, which Medina describes as effectively rendering it moot (Medina, 2026, p. 687), and this project treats the stricter route it left as no alternative mechanism. A coder could judge that second conjunct the other way, and the cell is marked as project coding for that reason. It was not a temporary provision. Its timing was tied to the pending petition. Medina’s analysis of those facts is that the timing, the partisan vote, the immediate effect and the sweeping scope all suggest a partisan purpose, and that the amendment’s content, which made it nearly impossible to declare the prime minister incapable, supports the conclusion that it was aimed at the current officeholder (Medina, 2026, p. 701). That analysis is his. The Court’s holding, as he reports it, is that the Knesset has no authority to alter the rules to benefit the current prime minister, and the Court let the amendment stand while ruling that it would take effect only after the next general elections and the formation of a new government, in *The Movement for Quality Government in Israel v. The Knesset*, HCJ 2412/23 (Jan. 3, 2024) (Medina, 2026, fn. 35 at p. 700; holding at p. 701). The expedited-process cell reads Yes on the strength of the research notes, which report ten days from first reading to final vote, and it is marked provisional because the article does not say so. The deviation cell reads Limited. That value is this project’s coding. Medina assigns the Incapacity case no tier in words; he presents it as an example of the purpose constraint, noting that where doubt about purpose remains an amendment is invalid only if it also violates the content constraint (Medina, 2026, p. 700), and the value is this project’s reading of that structure.

The blind coders scored it differently on three cells, and the difference is the one the recoding section describes. From the enacted text and the explanatory notes as quoted in the press, they coded no named beneficiary, because the text names no officeholder; scope No, because the text redefines the incapacity determination and supplies its own mechanisms rather than removing it; and direction neutral, because the clause barring judicial review was dropped before passage. They left coalition-only blank, because the press gives the tally, 61 to 47, with conflicting lists of absentees and no breakdown of the votes in favor. So a row that the illustrative table scored on all four signs scores on two in the recoded table, and the recoded row is excluded from the test as uncoded.

Read together, the illustrative cells say that this amendment was deferred because of its purpose. That is the pattern the decision-rule claim predicts. It is also a pattern the elite-interest claim can accommodate, because the amendment served a prime minister of the right, so the two claims part company only on rows where the Court’s signs and the enactment’s direction point different ways. One row cannot decide the test. A complete table can, if it contains rows of that kind, and that is why the table has to be complete and why T1 names an inconclusive outcome in advance.

Now take the first row, Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty of 1992. In *United Mizrahi Bank v. Migdal Cooperative Village*, CA 6821/93 (1995), the Court treated this enactment as the one that gave it the authority to review ordinary legislation for violations of rights (Medina, 2026, pp. 692–695). The row is marked as the must-match control. The term is borrowed from experimental practice, where a control is a case whose answer is known in advance and is used to check that the scoring works before the scoring is trusted on cases whose answer is disputed. This enactment entrenched a norm that was already part of the unwritten constitution, the lowest of the deviation steps, and it passed with support from both coalition and opposition members (Medina, 2026, pp. 684, 694). The research notes add that it had an ordinary eleven-month passage, which the row carries as a provisional value, and that its sponsors came from both sides. The row’s named-beneficiary cell is coded No as this project’s judgment that a bill of rights sponsored across the aisle addressed no specific interest of the sitting government; cross-party sponsorship is weak evidence for that, and the coding is open to challenge. The notes also infer, from the absence of a commencement clause, that it took effect on publication. Issue 1 asked whether a statutory default turns that inference into a rule, and the check found none for Knesset statutes: the Interpretation Law of 1981 fixes only the hour of commencement, the Transition Law of 1949 fixes a ten-day deadline for publication, and the publication-day defaults in the Law and Administration Ordinance of 1948 and the Interpretation Ordinance are written for ordinances and regulations. Under the issue’s stopping rule the cell was blank while the question was open. The check found no default, and the note’s change log item (aa) then adopted a coding convention: an enactment with no commencement clause is coded as taking effect on publication, “by absence of a clause”, which is the practice the research notes record. The 1992, 2016 and 2018 cells are coded that way and marked provisional, and the control’s criterion is restated to match, as the control paragraph below explains. In the recoded table the blind coders gave the row No on beneficiary, Yes on immediate effect by absence of a clause, No on expedited, Entrenches, and liberal in direction; they left coalition-only blank, because no permitted source gave a faction breakdown of the vote of 17 March 1992, which is why the row is excluded from the test as uncoded although it passes the control criterion on the cells in hand.

The control test was fixed in advance, in T1: this row must score low on every abuse indicator and be coded valid. The criterion as first stated could not be met by this row: it took effect on publication, like most Israeli laws, so it scores on immediate effect. The note’s change log item (aa) records the choice: the control must not satisfy the four-sign conjunction and must be coded Not adverse, because immediate effect is a feature of ordinary lawmaking and a sign only in the combination the Court states. On that criterion the row passes on the recoded cells: one sign of four, no beneficiary, not expedited, Not adverse, and a blank on coalition-only, which cannot satisfy the conjunction but is a gap in the record rather than a value. Its litigation-timing cell is No. On scope and temporary provision it is No on both, as a control should be. Before item (aa) the criterion as first stated would have failed on the immediate-effect cell once that cell was coded Yes, and the note’s change log recorded that as a deviation to be resolved before further coding, because a criterion adjusted after seeing the data is no longer a test. Two courses were open. The first kept the criterion as stated, in which case the immediate-effect indicator, as defined, failed the control and the indicator set needed revision before T1 ran. The second revised the criterion to match Medina’s reported guideline, which treats the four signs together and as a rebuttable presumption (the amendment is “likely” abusive “unless proven otherwise”), in which case the control must not satisfy all four together. Item (aa) took the second course. Under the first course a failed sign is a fault in the indicator; under the second, a coding that made this row satisfy all four signs would be a fault in the coding.

A blank cell means that the value has not yet been found in a source the row can cite. A blank stays blank until a source is found, because a value filled from what seems likely would carry the coder’s expectation into the data, and the test would then be measuring that expectation. The open questions about provisional and blank values are tracked as issues in the repository on GitHub, labeled `verification`, and each issue states what source would settle it and what to write if no source can be found. In the recoded table the blanks are the four coalition-only cells and the 1969 financing law’s majority cell, each with the search that failed described in the coders’ files, and a row with a blank among the four signs, the tier or the direction is excluded from the test as uncoded rather than classed.

## How it grows and how it gets frozen

The table grows in three ways. It gains rows. The aim is every Basic Law enactment or amendment since 1958 that was challenged before the Court or whose binding force the Court decided, the ordinary statutes the Court measured against Article 4, and a comparison sample of unchallenged Basic Law enactments and amendments taken as every third one in chronological order, so that the test can see what an unchallenged enactment looks like on the same columns. It gains columns for description: the opposition’s share of the vote as a number, the number of days from first reading to final vote, and a link for every value; the direction and targets-judiciary columns were coded blind on 22 October 2026 and will be coded the same way for every new row. And it gains rows for the enactments of 2025 and 2026 on which the Court has not yet ruled, with the outcome class Pending until it does.

Two of those enactments were written to take effect later: the March 2025 amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary, which takes effect from the next Knesset and will be a row, and the July 2026 law on the attorney general’s opinions, which takes effect on 1 January 2027 and, being an ordinary statute, will not (the sources are in the research folder). Delayed effect is the alternative to immediate effect that the article lists among the factors (Medina, 2026, pp. 700, 708). The research notes record, as an observation made after the events and therefore exploratory, that this design does not score on two of the six signs. The same 2025 amendment bears on the note’s conjecture C-model. C-model forbids the coalition re-using an instrument the Court has closed, and the March 2025 amendment re-used the Basic Law amendment instrument after the Court’s judgment on the Reasonableness amendment. The note’s ontology lists “Basic Law amendment” as one instrument, and its matrix M2 uses the same list without its last item, interpretation. On that list the 2025 amendment is a re-use, and so, on the same reading, were the 2016, 2018 and 2020 enactments after the Court’s warnings of 2011 and 2017 and both 2023 amendments after the Court’s 2021 ruling in Shafir. What C-model does not define is what it means for the Court to have closed an instrument: the whole class, or the class as used against one protected value. Until the note settles that, the 2025 amendment and the earlier re-uses stand as candidate refutations for test T4, and the change log records them.

Before any analysis runs on the table, the version it runs on will be frozen as a tagged release (a named snapshot of the whole repository at one moment, which is never changed afterward), and the analysis will cite the tag. The reason is the one the note gives for pre-registering its tests (recording the plan, and the data it will run on, before any result is seen). A dataset that can be adjusted after the result is seen can be adjusted until it gives the result the analyst wanted, and no reader can tell. A tag makes any later adjustment visible. Every change to the table after a tag is a commit with a message, and substantive changes are also recorded in the change log at the end of the note, with the date and the reason.

## How to criticize it

A row is refuted by a better source. The way to refute one is to open an issue that names the row, the cell, the source, and what the source says. Disagreement with how a cell was coded is welcome on the same terms: state the value you think is right and the authority for it. Three kinds of criticism would be especially useful. The first is a case we have missed, because the table’s coverage can only be as good as the search behind it, and that search was done without access to the Knesset’s own website. The second is a showing that one of the six signs is not stated or used by the Court as the table assumes, which would be a criticism of the test’s design, and it would need to be answered before T1 runs. The third is a row where the signs and the enactment’s direction point different ways, because rows of that kind are the ones that can decide the test.
