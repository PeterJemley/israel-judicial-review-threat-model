# The M4 codebook

## What this table is

This folder holds one data table, `m4-codebook.csv`, and this explanation of it. The table has one row for each of a set of enactments drawn from Barak Medina’s account of how the Israeli Supreme Court polices the Knesset’s power to make constitutional rules. Medina is a professor of law at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, and his account is the article that the conjecture note at the root of this repository examines (Medina, 2026). The Knesset is Israel’s parliament, with 120 members. The Court, when it reviews the acts of public bodies, sits as the High Court of Justice.

Most rows are Basic Laws (the statutes that together serve Israel in place of a single written constitution) or amendments to them that were challenged before the Court. Three rows are different. The first row, Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty of 1992, is in the table because the Court recognized it as binding in a civil appeal, and that recognition is the benchmark for what a valid constitutional enactment looks like; its row is marked as the control in the `case` column. The election-financing statute of 1969 was an ordinary statute. It had not passed with the sixty-one votes that Article 4 of Basic Law: The Knesset requires for a departure from equal elections, and the Court struck it; Medina stresses that the ground was the equality principle in Article 4 and not the vote count alone (Medina, 2026, p. 693). The enactment behind the 1977 Resler case needed checking. Medina cites Resler, with the signal “see, e.g.”, for the proposition that when the law previously declared invalid was re-enacted with the Article 4 majority the Court refrained from striking it (Medina, 2026, pp. 693–694). Two other sources describe the case differently and agree with one another. The Court’s own account, in its 1981 judgment in Agudat Derekh Eretz, is that Resler held that the Elections (Confirmation of Validity of Laws) Law of 1969 had validly ratified the election laws by absolute majority, and that the 1969 law said nothing about whether those laws met the equality principle. Klinghoffer’s 1983 article, as recorded in the research notes from its abstract page and its sixth footnote (the full article was not read), says the provisions challenged in Resler were in the Elections (Modes of Propaganda) Law of 1959, which governs election propaganda including broadcasting time, and that the Court refused to decide the equality question because the 1969 law had excluded it from review. A third source, Gal Dor’s 2000 article, does not appear to discuss Resler (the search and its limits are in the research log) but confirms that the 1969 law passed by special majority within two days, less than two weeks after Bergman. The row has been recoded on those sources, with the Hebrew judgment still unread, and the sources are listed in the row and in the research log. The `enactment_type` column records which rows are ordinary statutes.

The set is not yet complete even for the article. The 1996 Meatrael case, on an override clause added in 1994 to Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation, has a row with most cells blank (Medina, 2026, pp. 697–698). The three cases that followed Bergman on election rules (Medina, 2026, p. 693) have no rows yet.

Each row records how the enactment scores on signs that the Court, as Medina reports it, uses when it decides whether the Knesset abused its constituent power (the Court’s term for the power to make or change constitutional rules, as distinct from ordinary lawmaking). The table is called a codebook because, in social science, a codebook is the document that defines each variable in a dataset and the values it may take. It is called M4 because that is its label in the note’s appendix of matrices.

Three facts about Israel’s system explain why the table is worth keeping. Israel’s constitution is a hybrid of written and unwritten elements, and the written part, the Basic Laws, has been enacted one law at a time since 1958. The Knesset can enact or amend a Basic Law by a simple majority, through the same process it uses for an ordinary statute (Medina, 2026, pp. 683–684), apart from any provision that requires sixty-one votes to amend; the article discusses one, Article 4 of Basic Law: The Knesset, adopted in 1958 and the only such provision among the Basic Laws before 1992 (Medina, 2026, pp. 690, 693). And because the government of the day controls the Knesset in Israel’s parliamentary system, the body that changes the constitutional rules is the body those rules are meant to restrain (Medina, 2026, pp. 684, 690, 699).

## Why it exists

The note makes a claim that only data can test. The claim is that the Court’s doctrine on constitutional amendments is a real decision rule: an amendment’s fate depends on how far it departs from Israel’s unwritten constitution (the principles the Court treats as fundamental, among them free and equal elections, the rule of law, the separation of powers, judicial independence, and the protection of human rights), on how broad the support for it was, and on whether its purpose was to serve the sitting government. The rival claim, which Medina’s article answers and which the note keeps as a live alternative, is that the doctrine is a tool by which a liberal elite protects its position, so that an amendment’s fate depends on its ideological direction. The note’s first test, T1, asks which claim the record supports.

This table is where that record will be kept. As committed today it holds nine rows: eight drawn from the enactments Medina’s article discusses or cites, and the 1969 ratification law, which the article does not name. Most values were transcribed from the note’s matrix labelled M4; some were changed or blanked after the research notes were read, and the change log at the end of the note says in which categories. A few rows now carry sourced values the matrix did not have, but the table is still far from the record T1 needs. When it is complete and blind-coded, the claim can lose. Until then, it cannot.

## Why the columns are what they are

Six of the columns record signs of abuse drawn from Medina’s account of the Court. Four of them he gives as the Court’s guidelines, in a sentence of his own that reports them together: an amendment that addresses a specific interest of the current government, takes immediate effect, is supported only by members of the coalition (the parties that form the government and together hold a majority of seats), and is enacted through an expedited process is likely to be classified as an abuse of power unless proven otherwise (Medina, 2026, p. 699). The fifth, how sweeping the amendment’s scope was, Medina attributes to the Court’s reasoning in the Reasonableness case and also uses in his own analysis of the Incapacity case (Medina, 2026, p. 701). The sixth, how the amendment’s timing related to pending litigation, appears only in Medina’s narrative of the Incapacity case (Medina, 2026, pp. 687, 700, 701) and is attributed to the Court nowhere in the article. Elsewhere the article lists whether an amendment takes immediate or delayed effect as a factor in its own right (Medina, 2026, pp. 700, 708). The set of six is this project’s reading of Medina’s account. English translations of four judgments have been read for individual cells, and the research notes cite them: Bergman, Mizrahi Bank, Agudat Derekh Eretz, and the Reasonableness judgment. The six signs have not been checked as a set against any judgment, and that is open work.

The reason for taking the signs from the Court’s practice, as Medina reports it, deserves a plain statement. We did not invent five of the signs; the sixth, timing relative to litigation, is Medina’s observation, and we adopted it. We did choose which descriptors became columns, and we assigned every value, and both of those choices can be criticized. What the choice buys is narrower than fairness. A reader who suspects that the table was built to confirm the note can check each column against the Court’s judgments once they are read. But under the elite-interest claim the Court’s stated reasons are part of the tool, so a test that enters those reasons first and then asks whether ideological direction adds anything favors the decision-rule claim by construction wherever the reasons and direction move together. Direction has not been coded for any row, so whether they move together is not yet known. On the rows now in the table they appear to, if era is taken as a rough proxy: the rows that score on the signs are enactments of the governing coalitions of 2016 to 2023, the 1992 row was bipartisan, and the 1969 financing statute was a cross-party bill. The two budget rows of 2016 and 2020 may have no direction at all. The Resler check produced one row of a different shape: the 1969 ratification law was expedited and enacted in response to a judgment, yet it passed by absolute majority (its party breakdown is not known), and the Court declined review. Its discordance is between the signs and breadth of support, not between the signs and direction, and it does not enter the primary analysis, because the revised T1 treats a case in which review was foreclosed as a separate outcome class, consistent with the note’s record that a ratification which forecloses review does not test the sliding scale. So the table does not yet hold a row that can separate the two claims. T1 was revised on 22 October 2026 to handle this. Under the revision, every predictor is coded from legislative records by two coders who have not read the judgment, direction is coded blind, and only the rows where form and direction disagree are counted, in a two-sided sign test with a pre-stated minimum of six such rows before any result is claimed. The revision, its decision rule, and two controls, a shuffle of outcomes that the test must fail and a planted effect that it must detect, are in the note under the heading “T1 as revised on 2026-10-22”. The two claims can be stated without the ordering. The elite-interest claim predicts that outcomes track ideological direction and that the Court’s stated reasons track direction too. The decision-rule claim predicts that outcomes track the four form signs, and that direction has no effect once those are known. They differ only where the signs and direction diverge. The content axis of the sliding scale, the deviation tier, is outside the test, because a sharp deviation from the unwritten constitution and an illiberal direction are very nearly the same judgment. The inconclusive outcome is defined by the row count, fewer than six discordant rows, so no further computation is needed in advance. The eight rows coded on the day the note was written were coded before the revision, and all nine existing rows are to be recoded under it.

The remaining columns describe what the signs are applied to, and the values each may take are these.

- `case` names the enactment, and the case in which the Court dealt with it where that is the usual name.
- `year` is the year of enactment.
- `enactment_type` is Basic Law, Basic Law amendment, or Regular statute, with a note where the enactment was a temporary provision.
- The sixth sign, `litigation_timing`, records whether the enactment was passed while litigation it bears on was pending. An enactment passed in response to a judgment already given is coded as no pending litigation with the response noted, as in the 2016 and 1969 rows.
- `scope` is Yes if the enactment removes a check on the government or a protected right, or makes its exercise depend on the consent of the body it is meant to check, in either case without an alternative mechanism, and No otherwise. A one-time exemption from a rule is No. This is the definition adopted on 22 October 2026; it replaces an earlier column, `sweeping_scope`, whose values mixed breadth with duration and had no scoring threshold. Medina uses the word sweeping of the Reasonableness amendment, where he attributes it to the Court, and of the Incapacity amendment, in his own analysis (Medina, 2026, p. 701); both are Yes under the definition.
- `temporary_provision` is Yes if the enactment was passed as a temporary provision or applies to a single instance, and No otherwise. Medina describes both budget amendments as provisional measures and, in his own assessment of the 2020 case, treats the provisional nature as evidence of abuse (Medina, 2026, pp. 699–700). It is recorded as a sign, but it is not in the primary form score, which uses only the four signs Medina reports as the Court’s set.
- `targets_judiciary` is Yes if the enactment’s subject is judicial review, judicial appointments or judicial independence, and No otherwise. It exists so that the rows used to test the institutional self-interest rival can be identified from the table.
- `direction` is Illiberal if the enactment’s text or stated purpose moves the constitutional order toward preferring Jewish identity over equal citizenship, restricts the rights of minorities or women, or narrows judicial review or judicial independence; Liberal if it moves the other way; and Neutral if it has no identity or rights content, as with fiscal, procedural or housekeeping measures. Every cell is blank until two coders who have not read the judgments or this repository’s notes have coded it.
- `deviation_tier` records how far the enactment departs from the unwritten constitution, in the steps of the sliding scale that Medina reads in the Court’s approach (under which the greater an amendment’s departure, the broader the support it needs). The values are Entrenches, Limited, and Sharp. Entrenches is Medina’s own description of the 1992 Basic Law (Medina, 2026, p. 684). Limited and Sharp follow his distinction between a limited contradiction and a sharp one (Medina, 2026, pp. 684, 702). A first-day label, Near sharp, was used for the Nation-State Basic Law on the strength of Medina’s conjecture that the Court affirmed the limit on the Knesset’s power “likely because” that law came close to the threshold (Medina, 2026, p. 698); it was retired on 22 October 2026, and a blind coder will choose Limited or Sharp. Every other tier value is this project’s coding, marked so in the cell. Every tier value except the 1992 row’s was assigned with knowledge of the outcome the Court reached. That is a defect for T1, because a predictor derived from the outcome cannot test it. T1 requires two coders blind to outcome, and the current tier values are illustrative until they have been recoded that way.
- `outcome` records what the Court did.
- `source` records where each row’s values came from.

Every value in the table belongs to one of three classes. A value from the article carries no marker in the cell and is cited by page in the row’s `source` column. A value from the research notes, or from a source read in the verification pass, carries the word provisional and names the research item or the source. A value that is this project’s own judgment carries the words “this project’s coding”. A blank cell is a value that has not been found in a source the row can cite.

The contested variable, ideological direction, is now a column with a definition and no values. It stays empty until it has been coded blind, so that its coding can be criticized on its own, apart from the Court’s columns.

## How to read a row

Take the row for the Incapacity amendment. In March 2023 the Knesset amended Basic Law: The Government so that a prime minister could be declared incapable of serving only for medical reasons, and only by large majorities of both the cabinet and the Knesset. At the time, a petition (an application asking the Court to review a public act) was pending that asked the Court to declare Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu incapable because of a conflict of interest, and the amendment passed shortly before the Attorney General (the government’s chief legal adviser, who also represents the state before the Court) was due to respond to that petition (Medina, 2026, p. 687). The research notes record a conflict between two reports of that deadline, and the conflict is tracked as issue 5. One branch of it, a deadline of 12 March 2023, would put the passage after the deadline and would contradict the article’s “shortly before”, so the row’s timing value is marked provisional.

The row scores the amendment this way. The named beneficiary is the prime minister. It took immediate effect. Only coalition members voted for it. Its scope is Yes under the definition adopted on 22 October, because it made the incapacity mechanism depend on large majorities of the cabinet the prime minister leads, with no alternative mechanism. It was not a temporary provision. Its timing was tied to the pending petition. Medina’s analysis of those facts is that the timing, the partisan vote, the immediate effect and the sweeping scope all suggest a partisan purpose, and that the amendment’s content, which made it nearly impossible to declare the prime minister incapable, supports the conclusion that it was aimed at the current officeholder (Medina, 2026, p. 701). That analysis is his. The Court’s holding, as he reports it, is that the Knesset has no authority to alter the rules to benefit the current prime minister, and the Court let the amendment stand while ruling that it would take effect only after the next general elections and the formation of a new government, in *The Movement for Quality Government in Israel v. The Knesset*, HCJ 2412/23 (Jan. 3, 2024) (Medina, 2026, fn. 35 at p. 700; holding at p. 701). The expedited-process cell reads Yes on the strength of the research notes, which report ten days from first reading to final vote, and it is marked provisional because the article does not say so. The deviation cell reads Limited. That value is this project’s coding. Medina assigns the Incapacity case no tier in words; he presents it as an example of the purpose constraint, noting that where doubt about purpose remains an amendment is invalid only if it also violates the content constraint (Medina, 2026, p. 700), and the value is this project’s reading of that structure.

Read together, the cells say that this amendment was deferred because of its purpose. That is the pattern the decision-rule claim predicts. It is also a pattern the elite-interest claim can accommodate, because the amendment served a prime minister of the right, so the two claims part company only on rows where the Court’s signs and the enactment’s direction point different ways. One row cannot decide the test. A complete table can, if it contains rows of that kind, and that is why the table has to be complete and why T1 names an inconclusive outcome in advance.

Now take the first row, Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty of 1992. In *United Mizrahi Bank v. Migdal Cooperative Village*, CA 6821/93 (1995), the Court treated this enactment as the one that gave it the authority to review ordinary legislation for violations of rights (Medina, 2026, pp. 692–695). The row is marked as the must-match control. The term is borrowed from experimental practice, where a control is a case whose answer is known in advance and is used to check that the scoring works before the scoring is trusted on cases whose answer is disputed. This enactment entrenched a norm that was already part of the unwritten constitution, the lowest of the deviation steps, and it passed with support from both coalition and opposition members (Medina, 2026, pp. 684, 694). The research notes add that it had an ordinary eleven-month passage, which the row carries as a provisional value, and that its sponsors came from both sides. The row’s named-beneficiary cell is coded No as this project’s judgment that a bill of rights sponsored across the aisle addressed no specific interest of the sitting government; cross-party sponsorship is weak evidence for that, and the coding is open to challenge. The notes also infer, from the absence of a commencement clause, that it took effect on publication. Issue 1 asked whether a statutory default turns that inference into a rule, and the check found none for Knesset statutes: the Interpretation Law of 1981 fixes only the hour of commencement, the Transition Law of 1949 fixes a ten-day deadline for publication, and the publication-day defaults in the Law and Administration Ordinance of 1948 and the Interpretation Ordinance are written for ordinances and regulations. Under the issue’s stopping rule the cell is blank and the inference stays in the research notes. The same treatment is applied to the 2018 row, which the issue names, and to the 2016 row, which rested on the same inference and is noted in a comment on the issue.

The control test was fixed in advance, in T1: this row must score low on every abuse indicator and be coded valid. Whether it does is not yet known. Its immediate-effect and litigation-timing cells are blank, for the reasons given above and below. On scope and temporary provision it is No on both, as a control should be. If the immediate-effect cell is later coded Yes, the row fails the criterion as stated on one indicator. The note’s change log records that possibility as a deviation to be resolved before further coding, because a criterion adjusted after seeing the data is no longer a test. Two courses would then be open. The first course keeps the criterion as stated, in which case the immediate-effect indicator, as currently defined, fails the control and the indicator set needs revision before T1 runs. The second course revises the criterion to match Medina’s reported guideline, which treats the four signs together and as a rebuttable presumption (the amendment is “likely” abusive “unless proven otherwise”), in which case the control must not satisfy all four together. Under the first course a failed sign is a fault in the indicator; under the second, a coding that made this row satisfy all four signs would be a fault in the coding.

A blank cell means that the value has not yet been found in a source the row can cite. A blank stays blank until a source is found, because a value filled from what seems likely would carry the coder’s expectation into the data, and the test would then be measuring that expectation. The open questions about provisional and blank values are tracked as issues in the repository on GitHub, labeled `verification`, and each issue states what source would settle it and what to write if no source can be found. A blank with no issue has not been researched, apart from the 1992 litigation cell, for which the notes report that no source was found.

## How it grows and how it gets frozen

The table grows in three ways. It gains rows. The aim is every Basic Law enactment or amendment since 1958 that reached the Court, the ordinary statutes the Court measured against Article 4, and a comparison sample of unchallenged Basic Law enactments taken as every third one in chronological order, so that the test can see what an unchallenged enactment looks like on the same columns. It gains columns for description: the opposition’s share of the vote as a number, the number of days from first reading to final vote, and a link for every value; the direction and targets-judiciary columns exist and wait for blind coding. And it gains a second table, for the enactments of 2025 and 2026 on which the Court has not yet ruled.

Two of those enactments were written to take effect later: the March 2025 amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary, which takes effect from the next Knesset, and the July 2026 law on the attorney general’s opinions, which takes effect on 1 January 2027 (the sources are in the research folder). Delayed effect is the alternative to immediate effect that the article lists among the factors (Medina, 2026, pp. 700, 708). The research notes record, as an observation made after the events and therefore exploratory, that this design does not score on two of the six signs. The same 2025 amendment bears on the note’s conjecture C-model. C-model forbids the coalition re-using an instrument the Court has closed, and the March 2025 amendment re-used the Basic Law amendment instrument after the Court’s judgment on the Reasonableness amendment. The note’s ontology lists “Basic Law amendment” as one instrument, and its matrix M2 uses the same list without its last item, interpretation. On that list the 2025 amendment is a re-use, and so, on the same reading, were the 2016, 2018 and 2020 enactments after the Court’s warnings of 2011 and 2017 and both 2023 amendments after the Court’s 2021 ruling in Shafir. What C-model does not define is what it means for the Court to have closed an instrument: the whole class, or the class as used against one protected value. Until the note settles that, the 2025 amendment and the earlier re-uses stand as candidate refutations for test T4, and the change log records them.

Before any analysis runs on the table, the version it runs on will be frozen as a tagged release (a named snapshot of the whole repository at one moment, which is never changed afterward), and the analysis will cite the tag. The reason is the one the note gives for pre-registering its tests (recording the plan, and the data it will run on, before any result is seen). A dataset that can be adjusted after the result is seen can be adjusted until it gives the result the analyst wanted, and no reader can tell. A tag makes any later adjustment visible. Every change to the table after a tag is a commit with a message, and substantive changes are also recorded in the change log at the end of the note, with the date and the reason.

## How to criticize it

A row is refuted by a better source. The way to refute one is to open an issue that names the row, the cell, the source, and what the source says. Disagreement with how a cell was coded is welcome on the same terms: state the value you think is right and the authority for it. Three kinds of criticism would be especially useful. The first is a case we have missed, because the table’s coverage can only be as good as the search behind it, and that search was done without access to the Knesset’s own website. The second is a showing that one of the six signs is not stated or used by the Court as the table assumes, which would be a criticism of the test’s design, and it would need to be answered before T1 runs. The third is a row where the signs and the enactment’s direction point different ways, because rows of that kind are the ones that can decide the test.
