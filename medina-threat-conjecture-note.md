# Conjecture note: Medina's existential-threat claim under Capability × Intent

Date: 2026-10-21    Status: conjecture. T1 has been run once, on nine rows, and was inconclusive and exploratory; T2 to T4 have not been run. Two exploratory refutations are recorded (change log (ar)).

Source under study: Barak Medina, "On constitutional identity, democratic legitimacy, and judicial review in times of democratic backsliding: The case of Israel," I·CON 24(2) 681–708 (2026), https://doi.org/10.1093/icon/moag052. Page references are to the journal pagination.

- **Labels beginning R-**, such as R-actor, are readings: five ways of reading what the threat claim means. They are defined in "Readings". Here the R stands for reading.

## The question

As asked. Assume Threat = Capability × Intent. What are the threats Medina means when he writes, at p. 685, "These are not threats to private interests or ideologies of the liberal elite in Israel. Rather, it is an imminent risk to Israel's existence as a democracy, and, in fact, to Israel's very existence"? How should they be put into an ontology, or into matrices, for a variety of analyses?

Restated precisely. The sentence makes three separable claims, and the formula raises a fourth.

- Claim A (regime). Enacting the 2023 "Legal Reform" would put at imminent risk what the sentence's "These" refers back to at p. 685: "the right to vote, treating all citizens as equals, and preserving at least a minimal form of separation of powers". The institutional checks, chiefly the Court's review, are the means by which those values are protected; the values are what is threatened. For coding, this note uses the fuller list of five values the Court gave in Hasson (p. 698), which contains Medina's three: free and equal elections, core human rights and equality, separation of powers, rule of law, an independent judiciary. "Imminent" is part of the claim: on this project's reading, the loss would follow enactment within the enacting government's term rather than through slow erosion. No test in T1 to T4 bears on Claim A, and its conjecture C-A cannot be tested on the present record (see "What this note does not claim").
- Claim B (state). The loss of those protections is an imminent threat to the survival of the State of Israel itself.
- Claim C (not elite interest). Two claims sit here, and this note separates them. The p. 685 sentence says the threats are not to "private interests or ideologies of the liberal elite": a claim about what the 2023 plan attacks, which T2's catalogue bears on. At p. 684, answering the critique he cites at fn. 4, Medina defends the Court's doctrine: it is not a device for preserving the hegemony of a liberal elite. Claim C, as tested in T1, is the p. 684 claim. T1 cannot reach the p. 685 sentence, because it does not test the content constraint on identity and rights values ("What this revision tests"). Reading the p. 685 sentence as Medina's reply to the p. 684 critique is this note's interpretation (issue 21).
- Claim D (model). Capability × Intent, a product of two actor-level quantities, adequately describes the threat the paper describes.

## What the evidence already says

Results already in hand from the paper, failures first.

1. The coalition's formal capability did not convert into enacted reform. After forty weeks of protest, reservists' declarations that they "would withdraw from service if the government implemented its plan" (p. 686), and business and allied pressure, the government "did not propose the full plan for a final vote … and delayed the majority of the legislative process" (p. 687); "the subsequent war halted" its plans (p. 687). Bears on the actor reading (R-actor): capability must be measured net of resistance.
2. Two amendments passed anyway, both coalition-only and with immediate effect (p. 687). Bears on the actor reading (R-actor) and the doctrine reading (R-doctrine): residual capability stayed high for narrow instruments.
3. Both were neutralised by the Court. Reasonableness was struck (pp. 701–702); Incapacity was delayed to the next Knesset (pp. 700–701). Bears on the doctrine reading (R-doctrine): the two rulings are consistent with the sliding scale by construction, since Medina builds the scale from these rulings; they cannot test it. Medina presents Incapacity as "A notable example of implementing" the purpose constraint (p. 700), not the scale.
4. Hasson (2021) declared the identity limit but avoided applying it, using interpretation instead (p. 698). Bears on the doctrine reading (R-doctrine): the Court prefers soft constraints when they are available.
5. Medina reads Resler (1977) as the Court refraining from striking a law that had been re-enacted with the required majority (p. 694). On checking (change log, 2026-10-22), the Court's own account is that Resler held the 1969 Confirmation of Validity of Laws Law validly ratified the election laws by absolute majority and did not reach the equality question. Bears on Claim C: an absolute-majority ratification that forecloses review is consistent with the sliding scale but does not test it; under the revised T1 the row is an Article 4 row reported descriptively, not a primary-analysis row (change log (aa)).
6. The Knesset decided in 1950 to enact Basic Laws one at a time (p. 689). Medina writes that in the early decades a tradition of adopting constitutional norms through a bipartisan process mitigated the power to amend them by simple majority, and that in recent years, especially since the government elected at the end of 2022, the power has been exploited for immediate political purposes and ideological change (p. 708). Bears on the structure reading (R-structure) and Claim D: high capability at low intent in the early decades, with intent rising before 2022 (item 10).
7. The government returned to the plan in 2025 (p. 687). Bears on every reading: the threat was suspended, not removed.
8. Nothing in the paper tests any mechanism of the chain reading (R-chain). Bears on Claim B: on the security reading (R-chain) it is asserted, not argued; on the identity reading (R-identity) it restates Claim A.
9. After the events the article reports (no source gives its cut-off; it mentions the government's 2025 return to the plan, p. 687), the coalition re-used the Basic Law amendment instrument in March 2025 with deferred effect; the Court held the Shin Bet chief's dismissal unlawful after the government had rescinded it and froze, then annulled, the attorney general's dismissal, and in May 2026 the Court ordered the Judicial Selection Committee convened (research/2025-2026-timeline.md, added 2026-10-22). Bears on conjecture C-model: one more re-use of the Basic Law amendment instrument after the Court had closed it, on the class reading adopted under T4. On that reading the forbidding clause had already been refuted by the 2016, 2018, 2020 and 2023 enactments (change log (p), (t), (ar)); and on rival R3 (identity transformation): the elections committee's disqualification of the two Arab-majority lists, a candidate identity move for T2's catalogue, on motions including the governing party's, was reversed by the Court on 2 October 2026, an item on Medina's intent list at p. 685 that has recurred in earlier election cycles as well.
10. Medina does not date the exploitation of the amendment power from 2023. He writes that "In recent years, the Knesset has increasingly exploited its power" (p. 696), places the shift "In the past decade", with the 2018 Nation-State Basic Law "a pivotal piece of legislation" (p. 707), and says the power has been "discovered" and exploited "in recent years, especially since" the government elected at the end of 2022 (p. 708). The project's 2016, 2018 and 2020 rows carry abuse signs (form scores of 3, 2 and 4) and each met an adverse outcome as this project codes it (codebook/m4-codebook.csv, rows 5 to 7). Bears on the actor reading (R-actor): its prediction of near-zero threat before the January 2023 plan fails on the paper's own evidence; intent was rising for most of a decade before the plan (change log (ar)).

## Conjectures

Each conjecture states the property, the mechanism, the observable consequence, and what it forbids.

**C-A (Claim A holds).** Property: the 2023 package as a whole removes every horizontal check on the government. Mechanism: appointment control captures the reviewer; the unreasonableness amendment removes the standard of review; the incapacity amendment entrenches the incumbent (pp. 685–687, 700–702). Consequence: with the package in force, no institution could block an unlawful ministerial decision. Forbids: an enacted package coexisting with an effective horizontal check.

**C-doctrine (Claim C holds through the sliding scale).** Property: the Court's doctrine is a three-axis matrix, Deviation × Support × Purpose. Mechanism: the enforceable threshold rises with deviation from the unwritten constitution and falls with breadth and persistence of support; purpose is screened by the Court's four stated signs of abuse (F; T1 revised, Predictions). Consequence: invalidation is predicted by deviation tier and form indicators, with no residual effect of ideological direction. Forbids, restated from the prediction table of T1 revised: a Not adverse outcome for an enactment with all four of the Court's signs, for one that deviates sharply, or for one that deviates to a limited extent and passed by coalition-only vote; an Adverse outcome for an enactment that deviates to a limited extent, had opposition support and shows at most one sign, or for one that entrenches the protected values or engages none of them and shows at most three signs. Adverse includes deferral, narrowing interpretation and an invalidity warning, not striking alone (step 4).

**C-chain (Claim B needs an Israel-specific mechanism).** Property: Israel's security rests on a citizen-reservist army and on allied support, and both are conditional on democratic identity. Mechanism: reservists withhold service and allies withhold support when the state's democratic identity is in doubt. Consequence: measurable change in reservist attendance and allied policy within a year of enactment. Forbids: enactment with no change in either.

**C-model (Claim D fails as stated).** Property: the product form misdescribes this case. Mechanism: the actor's capability and the system's vulnerability are the same fact seen from two sides; intent and capability co-evolve because each ruling closes one instrument and the actor switches to another. Consequence: a correct model has a standing structural term, a time index, and non-additivity across instruments. Forbids: the coalition re-using the same instrument after the Court closed it, "closed" as defined under T4. On that definition the clause is refuted (T4; item 9).

A sketch that links C-doctrine to the capability term of Claim D, offered as a sketch and not a derivation: let capability at time t be coalition seats divided by the threshold the Court will enforce, and let that threshold be a function of deviation tier and breadth of support. The sliding scale is then the statement that the denominator rises with deviation. Purpose enters as a separate screen, not as a term in the ratio. That separation is this project's choice: pp. 700 and 708 tie purpose to deviation, which an interaction would capture (T1 revised, step 11b). The sketch has two limits. Support appears twice, in the numerator as seats and in the threshold, which falls with breadth of support. And nothing in it addresses instrument switching, which is C-model's mechanism, so it does not join the two conjectures.

## Prior art

Searched under "threat perception", "democratic backsliding" taxonomies, "abusive constitutionalism", and the Israeli overhaul literature.

- Singer (1958): conventional origin of threat as capability combined with intent in international relations.
- Landau-Wells (2024): two concepts hide under "threat perception", threat-as-danger and threat-as-signal; the assumption that intentions are inscrutable is itself untested.
- Cox (2008): the product formula Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Consequence fails to adjust for correlation among components, is non-additive, and misprices adaptive adversaries.
- Aven & Guikema (2015): adversary adaptation is the defining feature of adversarial risk.
- Grillo, Luo & Nalepa (2024): backsliding classified by restrainer (vertical or horizontal) crossed with target (electoral manipulation or executive aggrandizement).
- Bermeo (2016): executive aggrandizement as the dominant modern form; successor regimes shorter-lived and less authoritarian.
- Riedl, McCoy & Roberts (2024): pathways of legislative capture, plebiscitary override, executive power grabs, and elite collusion.
- Roznai, Dixon & Landau (2023): the democratic minimum core; abusive versus proto-abusive change; effect as prima facie evidence of intent.
- Roznai & Cohen (2023): the overhaul as a populist constitutional project; Israel's institutional vulnerability.
- Medina & Bloch (2023): structural versus substantive aspects of the identity transformation.
- Hostovsky Brandes & Roznai (2024): the salami tactic and the first-brick problem.
- Navot (2023): cumulative effect; the whole exceeds the sum of the parts.

The ontology in Appendix 1 is a specialisation of Grillo et al. and of Roznai, Dixon & Landau to the Israeli instrument set. It is not a new taxonomy.

## Proposed tests, in order

**T1. Code the amendments (C-doctrine against the elite thesis).** Single change: add ideological direction as a predictor alongside the form indicators. Unit: every Basic Law amendment and enactment since 1958 that reached the Court, plus a matched sample that did not. Variables: the six form indicators (named beneficiary, immediate effect, coalition-only vote, expedited process, sweeping scope, timing relative to litigation), opposition share of votes, deviation tier (entrenches, limited contradiction, sharp contradiction), ideological direction, and outcome (upheld, interpreted narrowly, invalidity warning, delayed, struck). Must-match control: the 1992 Human Dignity and Liberty enactment should score low on every abuse indicator and be coded valid. Must-fail control: shuffle the direction labels; direction must then predict nothing. Primary outcome: whether the outcome is predicted by indicators plus deviation tier with no residual effect of direction. Decision rule: supported if direction adds nothing once form and deviation are in; refuted if direction dominates; inconclusive if the sample cannot tell, which must be computed before coding because there are only a few dozen cases. Two coders, blind to outcome, with agreement reported.

**T2. Follow-on catalogue (rival R1 against rivals R3 and R4).** Single observation set: every government action taken under the unreasonableness amendment while it was in force, and every bill or policy advanced after the 2025 renewal. Code each move to one class, in this order of precedence: identity (an item on Medina's intent list at p. 685: Arab parties, religious norms, rights in the occupied territories); trial or attorney general (the prime minister's trial, or the attorney general's office); structural (any other change to an institution's powers or composition). Moves against the attorney general are therefore coded to the second class, not the third, so that R1 and R4 do not overlap. Decision rule: with fewer than five moves, Inconclusive. Otherwise R3 is supported if identity moves are at least one fifth of all moves; R1 if trial or attorney-general moves are at least half and there is no identity move; R4 if structural moves are at least half and there is no identity move; if both the R1 and R4 conditions hold, both are reported and neither is preferred; if no condition holds, the result is Mixed and the proportions are reported. The thresholds are this project's choices, set on 2026-10-29 knowing that the note's own records already hold candidate identity moves: the disqualification motions (item 9) and Basic Law: Torah Study (M1). Each enters the catalogue only if it meets the coding rule.

**T3. C-chain observables (Claim B).** For each mechanism fix the observable and the threshold before looking: reservist attendance by unit and month; allied policy events, not statements; capital flows and ratings; invocations of the Partition Plan by states or courts. Decision rule per row of M5.

T3 cannot be run on the present record, and C-chain is untestable for now. Its trigger, enactment of the package, has not happened. The only enactments, the Incapacity amendment of March 2023 and the Reasonableness amendment of July 2023, were followed within months by the mobilisation after 7 October 2023, which swamps every observable in M5. Before T3 is run, these are to be fixed and logged: the trigger, as the date an enactment that removes a check comes into force; the window, twelve months from that date; a numerical threshold for each row of M5, replacing its open-ended refuters; and a comparison that separates the enactment from a war, such as units or allies whose exposure to the enactment differs.

**T4. Instrument switching (C-model).** After each adverse ruling, record the next instrument the coalition used. C-model forbids re-use of the closed instrument. One re-use refutes the forbidding clause; repeated switching supports non-additivity.

**What "closed" means, and the clause's verdict (added 2026-10-29, after the data, and therefore exploratory).** The term was left undefined until now (issue 17), and undefined it kept the clause from being refuted. Two readings were available. On the class reading, an instrument is closed once the Court has issued an adverse ruling or an invalidity warning against any use of it. On the narrow reading, it is closed only when the Court has struck or suspended a specific measure, and re-use means enacting that measure again. On the class reading the Basic Law amendment was used again after the Court's 2011 warning in Bar-On (2016), after the 2017 warning on the two-year budget (2018 and 2020), after Shafir in 2021 (both 2023 amendments), and after the January 2024 rulings (March 2025). The clause is refuted on that reading. On the narrow reading no struck or suspended measure is found enacted again in the project's record to 21 October 2026 (research/historical-cases-findings.md; research/2025-2026-timeline.md). The clause survives on that reading only because it forbids almost nothing: a direct re-enactment, which the switching mechanism never needed to rule out. This note adopts the class reading and records the forbidding clause as refuted. C-model's other consequences, a standing structural term, a time index and non-additivity across instruments, are untested, and the switching half of T4 has not been run.

## T1 as revised on 2026-10-22

This revision was written after nine rows had been coded and read, and it is logged as a design change for that reason (change log, items (v) to (aa)). It replaces the analysis plan in T1 above. The aim is unchanged. The must-match control's criterion is restated in step 3 under the choice recorded in change log item (aa). The unit is widened, the comparison sample is drawn differently, the inconclusive decision moves from before coding to after predictor coding (step 7), and the primary analysis is restricted in time, all as stated below. Coding resumes only under this version, and the nine existing rows are recoded under it in full. That recoding was run on 2026-10-22. Its result under the rule is Inconclusive, on one discordant row against the minimum of six. The verdict was computed by code from N, K and the fixed predictions alone, although the person assembling the table had already seen the outcomes (change log (ab)). Because those nine rows shaped the rule (step 1), the run is exploratory, not pre-registered. The run, its deviations and its exploratory statistics are in research/coding-2026-10-22/results.md and in change log item (ab).

**Why the revision.** The form indicators were first coded from Medina's account of the Court's reasoning. Under the elite-interest rival the Court's stated reasons are rationalizations, so indicators taken from judgments cannot test that rival. And where every abusive-form enactment is also an illiberal one, no analysis can separate the two claims. The revision answers the first problem with a coding rule and the second with a choice of rows.

**What this revision tests, and what it cannot.** Medina's sliding scale pairs deviation from the unwritten constitution with breadth of support (pp. 702, 708). His text also ties purpose to deviation: p. 708 lists among the requirements that rise with deviation "the government's immediate interest in the amendment, whether the amendment takes effect immediately or is delayed", and p. 700 makes the purpose finding depend on content: "Where doubt remains, an amendment will be declared invalid only if it also violates the third constraint". The prediction table nonetheless treats the purpose signs (F) apart from the deviation tier, following the Court's statement of the signs as a set (p. 699). That separation is this project's design choice, and step 11b tests an interaction. Where the deviation concerns the identity and rights values of the unwritten constitution, a Sharp deviation and an Illiberal direction are very nearly the same judgment, and no row can separate the content constraint from the elite-interest rival. Where the deviation concerns the structural values, elections, separation of powers and the rule of law, an enactment can deviate sharply while being Neutral in direction, and such rows do separate the two. The revision tests the purpose constraint and p. 701's support condition, some opposition support, directly; it counts one opposition vote and does not measure breadth of support, and the content constraint only through rows of that second kind. The prediction table hardens Medina's permissive and probabilistic language ("likely", "may be permissible", "may suffice") into definite predictions, and its thresholds (F at most 1 as the absence of abusive purpose; one opposition vote as some opposition support) are this project's choices; a hit or a miss on such a row counts for or against the claim as this project has operationalized it, not for or against Medina's text.

**Roles and blinding.** Four roles are kept apart. The two coders code predictors from the sources in step 2 on a coding sheet that holds only enactment titles and dates and those sources, with comparison-sample rows unlabelled; they do not see this note, the codebook README, the research notes, the CSV, any judgment, or any case name. The adjudicator settles coder disagreements from the same sources and under the same restrictions, and is not the docket reader. The docket reader codes `litigation_timing` from court dockets and the Knesset's filings and then attaches the outcome column, in two separate passes, the second started only after the first's file is saved (a sequencing rule made explicit on 2026-10-22 after the lapse logged at change log (ab)); this person assembles the CSV. A fifth role, the circumstances reader, was added on 2026-10-22 (change log (ac)): this reader codes `situated_beneficiary` from dockets, filings and reporting dated no later than the day of passage, has not seen any outcome or judgment, and is not the docket reader. The author, who knows the outcomes of the nine rows already in hand, holds none of these roles. Withholding documents does not make a coder blind to outcomes already known from public life: the 2023 amendments and the Nation-State law are notorious, and later bills' explanatory notes refer to earlier rulings. Outcome-blindness is therefore not claimed for those rows. Nor is it claimed for the rows where the first run's roles reported exposure. All roles in that run were filled by language-model agents (results.md). The table was assembled not by the docket reader, as this protocol requires, but by an orchestrator who had seen the outcomes (change log (ab)). Both coders reported seeing court outcomes in search-result snippets for rows 2, 5, 6, 8 and 9, which include the one discordant row (2020) and step 11's second (2016). The circumstances reader under the tightened list reported outcomes reaching it for every row but the 1992 Basic Law and the 1969 ratification law. The first run's exploratory statistic S therefore rests entirely on rows with reported exposure. For them the judgment-call predictors that enter a prediction (`deviation_tier` and `direction`; `scope` is coded but enters no prediction, so flipping it would change nothing) are reported with a sensitivity analysis in which each such row's prediction is flipped in turn, and the primary result is reported with and without the notorious rows.

**Coding protocol.**

1. Unit. (a) Every Basic Law enactment or amendment since 1958 that has been challenged before the Court or whose binding force the Court has decided in a case applying it; (b) the ordinary statutes the Court measured against Article 4 of Basic Law: The Knesset; (c) a comparison sample of Basic Law enactments and amendments never challenged, taken as every third such enactment in chronological order. One row is one law titled Basic Law as passed at its third reading, however many Basic Laws it amends; temporary and single-instance provisions are in the frame; the order, the fixed start at position one, the frame-first sequence and the rule against back-filling are in change log item (ah). The 1992 Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty is in (a) by its second limb. The primary analysis uses the rows in (a) whose judgment came after the Mizrahi Bank decision of 1995, when the Court first held the Basic Laws binding on the legislature (pp. 692–695); Medina gives a 2017 case as "One example" of the purpose constraint (p. 699), and the project's notes record an earlier warning in Bar-On, 2011 (research/historical-cases-findings.md), and the test asks whether the Court's conduct since 1995 is consistent with the rule Medina states. Rows in (b), and rows in (a) decided before 1995, are coded on the same columns plus `required_majority_met` (Yes if the enactment passed with the majority Article 4 requires), and are reported descriptively under the Article 4 rule, which this project treats as a different doctrine (pp. 693–694); Medina presents Bergman as one of "Two seminal precedents" of a single approach (p. 693), so the separation is the project's. On the current illustrative cells the 1969 financing statute would be predicted Not adverse by the table and was Struck; that miss is disclosed, and it is the reason the Article 4 rows are not put through a table built for the constituent-power doctrine. Because the restriction to rows decided after 1995 was written after the nine rows had been seen (change log (aa)), the run on those nine rows is exploratory; the restriction is confirmatory only on rows coded for the first time under this revision. The comparison sample has the outcome Not challenged (step 4) and enters only the secondary analysis in step 14.
2. Sources for predictors. Knesset vote tables (the Open Knesset mirror of the Knesset's data), the Knesset's records of each government's composition, dates of first and final readings, the gazette text of the enactment, and the bill's explanatory notes. `litigation_timing` is outside the form score and is coded by the docket reader after the other predictors. `situated_beneficiary` is coded by the circumstances reader from court docket records of filings, from petitions and filings themselves, and from press reports, each dated no later than the day of passage; a source dated later may be used only to establish a fact dated no later than passage, and the reader stops at any description of what a court decided.
3. Predictors. Each predictor other than `litigation_timing` is coded by the two coders, with agreement reported per variable as Cohen's kappa and disagreements settled by the adjudicator before outcomes are attached. Where an enactment engages more than one protected value or moves in more than one direction, the coder records every value or axis engaged and codes the tier and the direction from the enactment's principal subject, stating which.
   - `named_beneficiary`: Yes if the enactment addresses a specific interest of the sitting government or an identified officeholder, from its text and explanatory notes; otherwise No.
   - `situated_beneficiary` (added 2026-10-22, change log (ac); closed list tightened the same day, change log (ae)): Yes if, on the day of passage, the enactment's operative provision would alter the legal position of an identifiable person then in office, or of the sitting government, in a matter then live, in a way that successors or other actors would not share; No otherwise. A matter is live only on facts dated no later than the day of passage, from this closed list: (i) a proceeding pending before a court concerning that person or that matter; (ii) a formal step already taken against that person or against the government's measure, such as an attorney general's opinion, an indictment, or a petition served; (iii) a fixed deadline that the enactment moves, where the deadline binds the sitting government or an officeholder; (iv) a single-instance or sunset clause, where the single instance or the sunset applies to the sitting government's own measure or term. A clause that touches every incumbent alike, or any government relying on existing law, satisfies no item. The alteration that must be unshared is the alteration in the live matter, not the form of the rule: a provision general in form is Yes where it has direct legal effect on a pending matter or formal step to which only the sitting person or government is party (clarified 2026-10-22, change log (af)). Judgments on the enactment, commentary, and the coder's belief about motive are excluded. An enactment whose operative provision is general, and whose connection to a live matter rests on motive alone, is No. The cell records the person or government, the matter, the admissible fact, its date, and which version of the list was applied. Coded by the circumstances reader, not by the coders. The list as first adopted, under which the nine rows' situated cells were coded on 2026-10-22, is in change log item (ac) and in the repository's history.
   - `immediate_effect`: Deferred if the text fixes effect after the next general election, or more than twelve months after publication; otherwise Yes, whether the text states effect on publication, on an earlier date (retroactive effect included), or within twelve months, or states no commencement clause at all, in which case the enactment is taken to have effect on publication, an inference recorded in the research notes (item 3) and the cell records "by absence of a clause". Deferred takes precedence where both could apply. Change log item (aa) records this convention as the choice under item (a): issue 1 found no statutory default, so the convention is a coding rule of this project, and with it the must-match control is scored on this indicator as Yes. The control criterion is accordingly restated: the control must not satisfy the four-sign conjunction of the first prediction-table row, and must be coded Not adverse. So restated it is close to tautological. In the blind record the 1992 row has `expedited` No and `coalition_only` blank, so it cannot satisfy the conjunction, and its outcome, Not adverse (1995), was known. Its result in the first run, not reported until 2026-10-29: met. It carries almost no information. Immediate effect is a feature of ordinary lawmaking and a sign of abuse only in the combination Medina reports the Court stating (p. 699).
   - `coalition_only`: Yes if no member of an opposition faction voted for the enactment at its final reading; No if at least one did; blank if the vote record is unavailable.
   - `expedited`: Yes if the final vote came within thirty days of the first reading, or if a special procedure shortened the committee stage; No otherwise. The number of days is recorded as well.
   - `scope`: Yes if the enactment removes a check on the government or a protected right, or renders it practically inoperative, and in either case provides no alternative mechanism; No otherwise. A one-time exemption from a rule is No. Both conjuncts are the coder's judgment.
   - `temporary_provision`: Yes if the enactment was passed as a temporary provision or applies to a single instance; No otherwise.
   - `litigation_timing`: Yes if litigation bearing on the enactment's subject was pending when it passed; No otherwise, with any response to a judgment already given noted separately.
   - `targets_judiciary`: Yes if the enactment's subject is judicial review, judicial appointments or judicial independence; No otherwise.
   - `required_majority_met`: for rows in (b) and pre-1995 rows in (a) only: Yes if the enactment passed with the majority Article 4 requires; No otherwise.
   - `deviation_tier`: None if the enactment's text engages none of the five protected values (free and equal elections, core human rights and equality, separation of powers, rule of law, independent judiciary); Entrenches if it entrenches one or more of them and contradicts none; Limited if it contradicts one to a limited extent; Sharp if it contradicts one sharply. Contradiction takes precedence over entrenchment, and the more severe contradiction over the less. The first-day label "Near sharp" is retired, and no row's option set is restricted in advance.
   - `direction`: Illiberal if the text or stated purpose moves the constitutional order toward preferring Jewish identity over equal citizenship, restricts the rights of minorities or women, or narrows judicial review or judicial independence; Liberal if it moves the other way on any of those; Neutral if it does none of these, which includes fiscal, procedural and electoral housekeeping measures.
4. Outcome, attached last by the docket reader. Adverse: the Court intervened against the enactment's validity or operation (struck, deferred, narrowed by interpretation, invalidity warning, declared ultra vires); where the Court both upheld an enactment and narrowed it, Adverse applies. Not adverse: the Court upheld or applied the enactment, or left it to operate after review on the merits. Not recognized: the Court declined to treat the enactment as binding (possible before 1995); excluded. Review foreclosed: the Court held that review of the enactment itself was excluded or declined jurisdiction over it. Not decided: a challenge withdrawn, dismissed as moot without a judgment on the merits, or dismissed as premature. Pending: a challenge filed but undecided. Not challenged: the comparison sample. **Analysis.**

5. The primary analysis uses the primary-analysis rows with outcome Adverse or Not adverse that are not excluded as uncoded. Undetermined and concordant rows stay in the permutation pool of step 6 but contribute nothing to S.
6. Primary test. The statistic S is the number of discordant rows whose outcome matches the decision-rule prediction minus the number that match the elite-interest prediction. Its reference distribution is the exact distribution of S when the outcome labels are permuted across all primary-analysis rows with every row's predictions held fixed; this distribution depends only on N (the number of primary rows), K (the number of Adverse outcomes among them), and the numbers of discordant rows the decision rule predicts Adverse and Not adverse, and it is enumerated exactly. The null distribution of S is not centred at zero when those two numbers differ and K over N differs from one half, so the two-sided p is twice the smaller of the two tail probabilities, P(S at least the observed value) and P(S at most the observed value), capped at one. The observed S is called above the null when it exceeds the mean of the null distribution and below it otherwise.
7. Minimum and detectability check, run before outcomes are revealed. At least six discordant rows are required. The docket reader then supplies N, the list of rows that are in the primary analysis, and K, the count of Adverse outcomes among them, without revealing any row's outcome. With these and the predictions fixed, the attainable range of S under the null is enumerated, and the two-sided p is computed at the largest attainable S and at the smallest. If the p at the largest attainable S is 0.05 or more, a Supported result is undetectable on this configuration; if the p at the smallest attainable S is 0.05 or more, a Refuted result is undetectable. If either is undetectable, the analysis is declared Inconclusive before the real outcomes are seen, and the configuration is reported. This is a detectability check, not a power computation.
8. Decision rule, applied only if step 7 passes. Supported: p below 0.05 and S above the null mean. Refuted: p below 0.05 and S below the null mean. Inconclusive: p of 0.05 or more. No other result is possible. Where step 7 fails, the observed S and p are reported as exploratory.
9. Implementation check, run before outcomes are revealed, with N, K and the predictions. The exact type-one error rate of the step 8 rule under the null is computed from the enumerated distribution and must be at or below 0.05. The code is also run on one thousand datasets with outcomes shuffled across the primary rows, and the fraction returning p below 0.05 must agree with the exact rate within 0.02. If either check fails, the implementation is wrong and the analysis is not run.
10. Secondary analysis, labelled exploratory: the primary test with the purpose-driven, content-driven and tier-and-support-driven discordant groups reported separately.
11. Secondary analysis, labelled exploratory: the primary test with the first row of the prediction table changed to F of 3 or more and the fifth row changed to F at most 2, the other rows unchanged.

## Appendix 1: Ontology

Classes

- Actor: governing coalition; Knesset as constituent assembly; Knesset as legislature; minister; prime minister as beneficiary; external actor (Hamas, kept only for contrast with Justice Baron's pairing at p. 682).
- Instrument: Basic Law amendment; regular statute; override clause; appointment control; administrative act; non-compliance with rulings; interpretation.
- Protected value: free and equal elections; core human rights and equality; separation of powers; rule of law; independent judiciary; the Jewish-and-democratic identity balance.
- Mechanism: remove the reviewer; remove the standard of review; change the reviewer's composition; entrench the incumbent; shift identity without formal amendment (pp. 685, 707).
- Constraint: soft or interpretive; legitimate purpose; identity; extra-legal (protest, reservists, business, allied governments).
- Indicator: named beneficiary; immediate effect; coalition-only vote; expedited process; sweeping scope; timing relative to litigation; opposition share.
- Consequence tier: regime (Claim A) or state (Claim B).
- Attributes on each threat instance: status (proposed, enacted, struck, delayed, moot); reversibility, this project's attribute (Medina's section 4.2, pp. 702–703, concerns the irreversibility of incorporating the unwritten constitution into Basic Laws, not the reversibility of a threat); date.

- An Actor uses an Instrument against a Protected value through a Mechanism.
- A Constraint blocks an Instrument.
- An Indicator evidences Intent. Roznai, Dixon & Landau (2023) treat effect as prima facie evidence of intent; this ontology keeps indicators and effects as separate classes so that the link between them can be tested rather than assumed.
