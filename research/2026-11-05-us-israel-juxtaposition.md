# The United States and Israel after their elections of autumn 2026: the checks set side by side

**What this is.** A research note, written to stand beside the evaluation of Amos Harel's Haaretz analysis of 4 November 2026 ([2026-11-05-harel-evaluation.md](2026-11-05-harel-evaluation.md)).

**Timing.** Within eight days in autumn 2026, both countries held elections that went against their governing parties:
- Israel's Knesset election of 27 October;
- the United States midterms of 3 November.

**What the note does.** It sets the checks on each executive side by side, to see which survive when the executive and the legislature are joined, as in Israel, and which only exist when they are separate, as in the United States. Nothing here is applied to the project's main note.

**What "the project" is.** This repository tests a sentence in Barak Medina's 2026 article (I·CON, p. 685): that the 2023 judicial plan posed "an imminent risk to Israel's existence as a democracy". It reads that claim through the threat-assessment formula Threat = Capability × Intent: what an actor can do, multiplied by what it means to do. The main document is [the note](../medina-threat-conjecture-note.md); the [README](../README.md) is the plain-language entry point. Project labels are explained where they appear.

**Sources.** The comparisons were prompted by a broadcast discussion of the US midterms that the author has checked. It is not cited; each fact below carries its own source. Tags:
- `[attested: ...]`: a text read for this note;
- `[press: ...]` and `[analysis: ...]`: a news report or an analysis piece, read;
- `[verified by the author, source not cited]`;
- `[run: ...]`: found in the author's legal-database run of 5 November 2026 ([legal-db-2026-11-05/](legal-db-2026-11-05/README.md)), with that run's own tag, and not re-read for this note;
- `[conjectural]`: this note's inference.

## Row by row

**1. Who certifies an election, and who can refuse to seat a winner**

- **United States:**
  - The Constitution makes "each House ... the judge of the elections, returns and qualifications of its own members" (Art. I, § 5). `[attested: Cornell LII, Constitution, Art. I]`
  - In *Roudebush v. Hartke*, 405 U.S. 15 (1972), the Court held that Indiana's recount of a 1970 Senate race, won by 4,383 votes, was "within the ambit of the broad powers delegated to the States by Art. I, § 4". It did not usurp the Senate, which "is free to accept or reject the apparent winner in either count, and, if it chooses, to conduct its own recount". `[attested: Cornell LII, 405 U.S. 15]`
  - Before a new House first meets, "the Clerk of the next preceding House of Representatives shall make a roll of the Representatives-elect", placing on it only those "whose credentials show that they were regularly elected in accordance with the laws of their States" (2 U.S.C. § 26). `[attested: Cornell LII, 2 U.S.C. § 26]`
  - The Clerk is one of the officers the House chooses (Art. I, § 2). `[attested: as above]`
  - The new Congress meets on 3 January (Twentieth Amendment, § 2). `[attested: Cornell LII, Amendment XX]`
- **Israel:**
  - On 4 November the Central Elections Committee published final results, finding no irregularities. `[press: Harel, Haaretz, 4 November 2026]`
  - Its chair, Justice Noam Sohlberg, had earlier asked the Shin Bet chief to investigate suspected foreign funding. `[press: Harel]`
  - The new Knesset is to be sworn in on 10 November. `[analysis: Keinon, Jerusalem Post, 1 November 2026]` A wire report gives the same date for its first session. `[run: attested, Lexis, MENAFN, 3 November 2026]`
  - This note has not examined who in Israeli law can refuse to seat a certified member. A search of English sources in Westlaw and Lexis on 5 November found nothing on the point. `[run: not found]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - In the United States, the last judge of an election is the chamber itself. So the point of risk is the chamber, or the officer who makes its roll, acting against a certified result.
  - In Israel, the point of risk the Harel evaluation identifies is earlier: the security service feeding claims to the election authority. `[conjectural]`
  - Both are routes for the same act, contesting a certified result. That act is the mechanism the Harel evaluation listed as missing from the project's catalogue of threats.

**2. Law enforcement and security services**

- **United States:**
  - The Constitution gives the Attorney General no stated role; the department's independence from the President rests on convention. `[conjectural]`
  - On 1 December 2020 the Attorney General told the Associated Press that the department had "not seen fraud on a scale that could have effected a different outcome". `[run: attested, Lexis, Michael Balsamo (AP), Spokesman-Review, 2 December 2020, at A3]`
  - He left office later that month. `[verified by the author, source not cited]`
- **Israel:**
  - The government's dismissal of Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara (August 2025) was frozen by the High Court and annulled 7 to 0 on 14 December 2025. `[attested: research/2025-2026-timeline.md]`
  - The Shin Bet chief's dismissal was frozen on 21 March 2025, the day after the cabinet's vote. `[press: Jerusalem Post, 21 March 2025]`
  - A law ending the binding status of the attorney general's opinions takes effect on 1 January 2027. `[attested: research/2025-2026-timeline.md]`
  - After the 2026 election, the attorney general held the Shin Bet chief's claims to a criminal standard of evidence. `[press: Harel]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - In both countries the heads of law enforcement can act as a check on the executive's account of an election.
  - In the United States that check rests on convention. In Israel it has rested on the Court's protection of the attorney general, and the 2026 law would narrow it from January. `[conjectural]`
  - The security service as an instrument of the executive is the other gap the Harel evaluation found in the project's catalogue.

**3. The legislature**

- **United States:**
  - "No money shall be drawn from the treasury, but in consequence of appropriations made by law" (Art. I, § 9).
  - A vetoed bill becomes law only "if approved by two thirds" of each House (Art. I, § 7). `[attested: Cornell LII, Art. I]`
  - On AP projections, the Democrats flipped at least six Senate seats and won the House, "taking full control over Congress". `[press: Jerusalem Post, 4 November 2026, citing AP projections]`
  - So the legislature becomes a separate check: on money, on legislation, and, through the Senate, on appointments. `[conjectural]`
- **Israel:**
  - Coalition discipline "generally ensures that the executive and legislative branches – the government and the Knesset, respectively – are on the same page". `[analysis: Keinon]`
  - Basic Laws can be amended by a simple majority (Medina, 2026, p. 708). This is the fact behind the project's model conjecture, its testable claim that the plain formula misdescribes Israel: what the coalition is able to do and where the system is open to attack are the same fact.
  - The 27 October election produced a deadlock. The opposition bloc's 48 seats, "together with the Arab parties' 19, give them the numbers to elect a replacement" for the Likud speaker, Amir Ohana. Keinon calls this "an unusually independent Knesset", at least for a time. `[analysis: Keinon]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - The United States has, by its structure, a legislature that can stand apart from the executive.
  - Israel may have one now only because of an electoral deadlock, and only until a government is formed.
  - If the Knesset elects a speaker against the outgoing government, capability and vulnerability separate in Israel for the first time in the project's record. That would bear directly on the model conjecture: the structural part of the threat would turn out to vary with time, not to be fixed. `[conjectural]`

**4. The courts**

- **United States:** federal courts issued more than 100 injunctions against the administration. The Solicitor General appealed only a handful to the Supreme Court, and the rest remain in force. `[verified by the author, source not cited]` A search of Westlaw and Lexis on 5 November did not find a written source for the figures. `[run: not found]`
- **Israel:**
  - The High Court struck the Reasonableness amendment and deferred the Incapacity amendment to the next Knesset, in January 2024. `[attested: codebook/m4-codebook.csv, rows 8 and 9]`
  - On 31 May 2026 it ordered the justice minister to convene the Judicial Selection Committee. He called the order "an apparently illegal decision". `[attested: research/2025-2026-timeline.md]`
- **What the contrast teaches:** in both systems the courts' orders work only as far as the executive complies. The project's matrix of constraints against instruments already records non-compliance as the one instrument no legal constraint reaches; only extra-legal pressure does. `[conjectural]`

**5. Judicial appointments**

- **United States:** the President appoints "judges of the Supreme Court" only "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate" (Art. II, § 2). `[attested: Cornell LII, Art. II]` With a Democratic Senate, a nominee must be acceptable to it. `[conjectural]`
- **Israel:**
  - The March 2025 amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary changes the committee that selects judges, and takes effect "on the day the 26th Knesset begins its term". Petitions against it are pending. `[attested: research/coding-2026-11-04-row10/]`
  - The Court issued conditional orders on 2 December 2025, requiring the Knesset and the government to explain why the law should not be struck down. Eleven justices heard the petitions on 21 June 2026. No ruling was reported through 5 November 2026. `[run: attested, Lexis, Jerusalem Post, 3 December 2025 and 27 July 2026]`
  - Unless the Court rules or issues an interim order first, the amendment takes effect when the new Knesset first meets, on 10 November. `[run: conjectural]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - Because of the deferral, the amendment does not show one of the Court's signs of abuse, an amendment taking effect at once for the government that passed it, which the project codes as "immediate effect".
  - The deferral now hands the new committee to whichever coalition and opposition the 2026 Knesset produces, which may not be the coalition that wrote it. `[conjectural]`

**6. A written constitution**

- **United States:** one document, amendable only by the supermajorities of Article V. Its protection depends on officials complying with it and with the courts. `[conjectural]`
- **Israel:** no single written constitution. The Basic Laws serve in its place, and most can be amended by a simple majority (Medina, 2026, p. 708).
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - The structural part of the threat is larger in Israel, because the rules themselves can be changed by the governing majority.
  - In both systems, the last line is compliance. That is where row 4 ended too. `[conjectural]`

**7. After power changes hands**

- **United States:** the House has "the sole power of impeachment". In the Senate, "no person shall be convicted without the concurrence of two thirds of the members present" (Art. I, §§ 2–3). `[attested: Cornell LII, Art. I]`
- **Israel:**
  - The opposition has said it would dismiss the Shin Bet chief if it forms a government. `[press: Harel]`
  - Keinon reports it could use a speaker of its own to advance:
    - a state commission of inquiry into 7 October;
    - a law on haredi conscription;
    - the rollback of overhaul legislation. `[analysis: Keinon]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - A successor's moves need the same scrutiny as the predecessor's. The project's planned catalogue of the government's moves now codes each move by its aim as well as its target, and that coding applies to any government.
  - Whether accountability for the previous government reads as accountability or as retaliation is, on the project's terms, a question about aims, settled by stated purpose or an act, not by commentary. `[conjectural]`

**8. A third model: the United Kingdom**

- **The model.** A returning officer certifies the result, and the House of Commons seats the member certified. Disputes go to an election court, not to the House.
- **An example.** When Tony Benn, by then Viscount Stansgate, won the Bristol South-East by-election of 1961, the election court held him disqualified as a peer. `[verified by the author, source not cited]` Justices Gorman and McNair held that he could not keep the seat after succeeding to his father's peerage, and that the runner-up took it without a new election. `[run: attested, Lexis, Tillman, 2025 Harv. J.L. & Pub. Pol'y Per Curiam 1, n.13]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - In this third arrangement, neither the chamber nor a security service decides who sits. An official certifies and a court decides disputes.
  - In Israel, the official who certifies is a Supreme Court justice. That puts Israel nearer this model than the American one, which is why his request to the Shin Bet matters. `[conjectural]`

## What the juxtaposition means for the project

- **The model conjecture gains a test it lacked.** The conjecture holds that in Israel what the coalition can do and where the system is open are the same fact. If the 26th Knesset elects a speaker against the outgoing government, the two separate, and the structural part of the threat proves to change with elections rather than to stand fixed. `[conjectural]`
- **The catalogue's gaps recur in both systems.** "Contesting a certified result" is a mechanism in both. The executive's use of law enforcement or security services is an instrument in both. A chamber judging its own members is a constraint the US has and Israel's catalogue does not record. All three are listed as candidates, not added.
- **Compliance is the floor in both.** Rows 4 and 6 end at the same place: courts and constitutions bind only as far as the executive complies. That is where the project's catalogue already places non-compliance, reached only by extra-legal pressure.
