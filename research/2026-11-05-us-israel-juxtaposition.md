# The United States and Israel after their elections of autumn 2026: the checks set side by side

**What this is.** A research note, written to stand beside the evaluation of Amos Harel's Haaretz analysis of 4 November 2026 ([2026-11-05-harel-evaluation.md](2026-11-05-harel-evaluation.md)).

**Author.** Peter Jemley, who maintains this project.

**What "the project" is.** This repository tests a sentence in Barak Medina's 2026 article in the *International Journal of Constitutional Law* (I·CON), p. 685: that the 2023 judicial plan posed "an imminent risk to Israel's existence as a democracy". It reads that claim through the threat-assessment formula Threat = Capability × Intent: what an actor can do, multiplied by what it means to do. The project's main document is [the note](../medina-threat-conjecture-note.md); the [README](../README.md) is the plain-language entry point. Project labels are explained where they appear.

**Timing.** A week apart in autumn 2026, both countries held elections that went against their governing parties:
- Israel's Knesset election of 27 October;
- the United States midterms of 3 November.

**What this comparison does.** It sets the checks on each executive side by side, to see which survive when the executive and the legislature are joined, as in Israel, and which only exist when they are separate, as in the United States. Nothing here is applied to the project's main note. Corrected on 5 November 2026 after an outside reader's review (the main note's change log, item (bg)).

**Sources.** The comparisons were prompted by a broadcast discussion of the US midterms that the project's author has checked. It is not cited; each fact below carries its own source. Claims for which no written source was found were cut on 6 November 2026. Tags:
- `[attested: ...]`: a text read for this note;
- `[press: ...]` and `[analysis: ...]`: a news report or an analysis piece, read;
- `[run: ...]`: found in the project author's legal-database run of 5 November 2026 ([legal-db-2026-11-05/](legal-db-2026-11-05/README.md)), with that run's own tag, and not re-read for this note;
- `[conjectural]`: this note's inference.

## The table

| Check | United States | Israel |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Who certifies an election, and who can refuse to seat a winner | The states run and count elections; each chamber of Congress is "the judge of the elections, returns and qualifications of its own members"; the Clerk of the outgoing House makes the roll of members-elect | The Central Elections Committee, chaired by a Supreme Court justice, certifies; the new Knesset is sworn in on 10 November |

Law enforcement and security services | The Attorney General's independence rests on convention; in 2020 the Attorney General, William Barr, said the department had not seen fraud on a scale that could change the result | The attorney general's dismissal was annulled by the Court; the Shin Bet chief, David Zini, raised foreign-funding claims in the week after the election |
| 3. The legislature | Separate from the executive: the purse, the veto and its two-thirds override, Senate consent; both chambers projected to pass to the Democrats | Ordinarily joined to the executive by coalition discipline; after 27 October, possibly an "unusually independent Knesset" |
| 4. The courts | Federal courts can enjoin the executive; figures on injunctions were cut for want of a written source | The High Court struck one 2023 amendment and deferred another, and ordered the judicial selection committee convened |
| 5. Judicial appointments | Need the Senate's consent | Set by the 2025 Judicial Selection amendment, which takes effect with the new Knesset |

| 7. After power changes hands | Impeachment needs two thirds of the senators present to convict | The opposition calls for the Shin Bet chief's dismissal and may roll back overhaul laws |

A third model, the United Kingdom (row 8 below): the returning officer's certificate, not the House of Commons, decides who is seated, and disputes go to an election court.

## Row by row

**1. Who certifies an election, and who can refuse to seat a winner**

- **United States:**
  - The Constitution makes "each House ... the judge of the elections, returns and qualifications of its own members" (Art. I, § 5). `[attested: Cornell LII, Constitution, Art. I]`
  - In *Roudebush v. Hartke*, 405 U.S. 15 (1972), the Court held that Indiana's recount of a 1970 Senate race, won by 4,383 votes, was "within the ambit of the broad powers delegated to the States by Art. I, § 4". It did not usurp the Senate, which "is free to accept or reject the apparent winner in either count, and, if it chooses, to conduct its own recount". `[attested: Cornell LII, 405 U.S. 15]`
  - Before a new House first meets, "the Clerk of the next preceding House of Representatives shall make a roll of the Representatives-elect", placing on it only those "whose credentials show that they were regularly elected in accordance with the laws of their States" (2 U.S.C. § 26). `[attested: Cornell LII, 2 U.S.C. § 26]`
  - The House "shall choose their speaker and other officers" (Art. I, § 2). `[attested: Cornell LII, Constitution, Art. I]` The Clerk is one of those officers by the House's practice. `[conjectural]`
  - The new Congress meets at noon on 3 January, "unless they shall by law appoint a different day" (Twentieth Amendment, § 2). `[attested: Cornell LII, Amendment XX]`
- **Israel:**
  - On 4 November the Central Elections Committee published final results, finding no irregularities. `[press: Harel, Haaretz, 4 November 2026]`
  - Its chair, Justice Noam Sohlberg, had earlier, on 29 October, instructed the Shin Bet chief to investigate suspected foreign funding. `[press: Harel]`
  - The new Knesset is to be sworn in on 10 November. `[analysis: Herb Keinon, Jerusalem Post, 1 November 2026]` A wire report gives the same date for its first session. `[run: attested, Lexis, MENAFN, 3 November 2026]`
  - This note has not examined who in Israeli law can refuse to seat a certified member. A search of English sources in Westlaw and Lexis on 5 November found nothing on the point. `[run: not found]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - In the United States, the last judge of an election is the chamber itself. So the point of risk is the chamber, or the officer who makes its roll, acting against a certified result.
  - In Israel, the point of risk the Harel evaluation identifies is earlier: the security service feeding claims to the election authority. `[conjectural]`
  - Both are routes for the same act, contesting a certified result. That act is the mechanism the Harel evaluation listed as missing from the project's catalogue of threats.

**2. Law enforcement and security services**

- **United States:**
  - The Constitution gives the Attorney General no stated role; the department's independence from the President rests on convention. `[conjectural]`
  - On 1 December 2020 the Attorney General, William Barr, told the Associated Press that the department had "not seen fraud on a scale that could have effected a different outcome". `[run: attested, Lexis, Michael Balsamo (AP), Spokesman-Review, 2 December 2020, at A3]`
- **Israel:**
  - The government's dismissal of Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara (August 2025) was frozen by the High Court (Israel's Supreme Court sitting as the High Court of Justice) and annulled 7 to 0 on 14 December 2025. `[attested: research/2025-2026-timeline.md]`
  - The dismissal of the then Shin Bet chief, Ronen Bar, was frozen on 21 March 2025, the day after the cabinet's vote. `[press: Jerusalem Post, 21 March 2025]` `[attested: research/2025-2026-timeline.md]` for his name.
  - A law ending the binding status of the attorney general's opinions passed on 15 July 2026 and takes effect on 1 January 2027. `[attested: research/2025-2026-timeline.md]`
  - After the 2026 election, the attorney general and the police restated to the current Shin Bet chief, David Zini, that any inquiry needs "reasonable suspicion of criminal offenses". `[press, anonymous: Harel]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - In both countries the heads of law enforcement can act as a check on the executive's account of an election.
  - In the United States that check rests on convention. In Israel it has rested on the Court's protection of the attorney general, and the July 2026 law would narrow it from January. `[conjectural]`
  - The security service as an instrument of the executive is the other gap the Harel evaluation found in the project's catalogue.

**3. The legislature**

- **United States:**
  - "No money shall be drawn from the treasury, but in consequence of appropriations made by law" (Art. I, § 9).
  - A vetoed bill becomes law only "if approved by two thirds" of each House (Art. I, § 7). `[attested: Cornell LII, Art. I]`
  - On AP projections, the Democrats flipped at least six Senate seats and won the House, "taking full control over Congress". `[press: Jerusalem Post, 4 November 2026, citing AP projections]`
  - So the legislature becomes a separate check: on money, on legislation, and, through the Senate, on appointments. `[conjectural]`
- **Israel:**
  - Coalition discipline "generally ensures that the executive and legislative branches – the government and the Knesset, respectively – are on the same page". `[analysis: Keinon]`
  - Most Basic Laws can be amended by a simple majority (Medina, 2026, p. 708). This is the fact behind the project's model conjecture, its testable claim that the plain formula misdescribes Israel: what the coalition is able to do and where the system is open to attack are the same fact.
  - The 27 October election left the outgoing coalition without a majority. The opposition bloc's 48 seats, "together with the Arab parties' 19, give them the numbers to elect a replacement" for the Likud speaker, Amir Ohana. Keinon calls this "an unusually independent Knesset", at least for a time. `[analysis: Keinon]` On those figures neither bloc reaches a majority of 61 without the Arab parties. `[conjectural]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - The United States has, by its structure, a legislature that can stand apart from the executive.
  - Israel may have one now only because no bloc has a majority, and only until a government is formed.
  - Suppose the Knesset elects a speaker against the outgoing government. Then what the outgoing coalition can do and where the system is open to attack would come apart, at least for a time. That would bear directly on the model conjecture: the part of the threat that comes from the system's standing design would turn out to vary with elections, not to be fixed. `[conjectural]`

**4. The courts**

- **United States:** federal courts can enjoin the executive, and the Solicitor General decides which orders to appeal. `[conjectural]` The figures a broadcast gave for injunctions issued against the administration and appealed were cut on 6 November 2026: a search of Westlaw and Lexis found no written source for them. `[run: not found]`
- **Israel:**
  - The High Court struck the Reasonableness amendment and deferred the Incapacity amendment to the next Knesset, in January 2024. `[attested: codebook/m4-codebook.csv, rows 8 and 9]`
  - On 31 May 2026 it ordered the justice minister to convene the Judicial Selection Committee. He called the order "an apparently illegal decision". `[attested: research/2025-2026-timeline.md]`
- **What the contrast teaches:** in both systems the courts' orders work only as far as the executive complies. The project's matrix of constraints against instruments already records non-compliance as the one instrument no legal constraint reaches; only extra-legal pressure does. `[conjectural]`

**5. Judicial appointments**

- **United States:** the President appoints "judges of the Supreme Court" only "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate" (Art. II, § 2). `[attested: Cornell LII, Art. II]` With a Democratic Senate, a nominee must be acceptable to it. `[conjectural]`
- **Israel:**
  - The March 2025 amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary changes the committee that selects judges, and takes effect "on the day the 26th Knesset begins its term". Petitions against it are pending. `[attested: research/coding-2026-11-04-row10/]`
  - The Court issued conditional orders on 2 December 2025, requiring the Knesset and the government to explain why the law should not be struck down. Eleven justices heard the petitions on 21 June 2026. `[run: attested, Lexis, Jerusalem Post, 3 December 2025 and 27 July 2026]`
  - A search of Lexis news reports through 5 November 2026 found no ruling. `[run: attested, Lexis, titles read to 5 November 2026]`
  - Unless the Court rules or issues an interim order first, the amendment takes effect when the new Knesset first meets, on 10 November. `[conjectural]`, following the run's own inference.
  - *Recorded on 10 November 2026, at a check-in the project's author scheduled for that day. Sources and gaps are listed in the Harel evaluation's addendum of the same date. The project's **codebook** ([codebook/m4-codebook.csv](../codebook/m4-codebook.csv)) is its table of Basic Laws challenged before the High Court, one numbered row per law. This amendment is row 10, and its outcome is coded **Pending**: no ruling yet.*
    - **No ruling and no interim order were found before the Knesset first met.**
      - The Jerusalem Post reports of 5, 9 and 10 November read for the check-in do not mention the petitions. They are political reports, not court reports. `[press: JPost, articles 913918, 914332, 914409, 914426 and live updates 914399]`
      - Web searches on 10 November found no report of a ruling or an interim order. `[search-lead]`
      - The Court's own docket was not searched, because the petitions' case numbers (HCJ, the High Court of Justice) have not been found.
    - **The 26th Knesset first met on 10 November.** Its first plenum session is recorded that day, and the table of Knesset terms marks it as current from that day. `[attested: Knesset open data, plenum session 2245487 and the table of Knesset terms]`
    - **So, by its text, the amendment took effect on 10 November.** No source read says that it is now treated as in force, or that the committee in its new form has been made up or has met. `[conjectural]`
    - **The Incapacity amendment applies from the same day.** Under the Court's January 2024 ruling, it applies from the start of the 26th Knesset. `[run: attested, Lexis, Jerusalem Post, 4 January 2024; research/frame/challenges.json]` It is codebook row 8, whose outcome is coded Adverse because the Court deferred it. `[attested: codebook/m4-codebook.csv, row 8]` That it now applies follows from the date. `[conjectural]`
    - **Repeal bills.** On the first day, Yashar submitted bills "aimed at repealing laws enacted as part of the outgoing government's judicial overhaul". `[press: JPost, live updates 914399, 10 November]` Yashar is Gadi Eisenkot's party, in the opposition bloc. The fuller report names repeal bills on the broadcasting law and on the attorney general's powers. Neither report names this amendment. `[press: JPost, 10 November, article 914426]`
    - **The codebook is unchanged.** Row 10 stays Pending.
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - Because the amendment starts only with the next Knesset, it does not show one of the Court's signs of abuse, an amendment taking effect at once for the government that passed it, which the project codes as "immediate effect".
  - That delayed start now hands the new committee to whichever coalition and opposition the 2026 Knesset produces, which may not be the coalition that wrote it. `[conjectural]`

**6. A written constitution**

- **United States:** one document, amendable only by the supermajorities of Article V. Its protection depends on officials complying with it and with the courts. `[conjectural]`
- **Israel:** no single written constitution. The Basic Laws serve in its place, and most can be amended by a simple majority (Medina, 2026, p. 708).
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - The structural part of the threat is larger in Israel, because the rules themselves can be changed by the governing majority.
  - In both systems, the last line is compliance. That is where row 4 ended too. `[conjectural]`

**7. After power changes hands**

- **United States:** the House has "the sole power of impeachment". In the Senate, "no person shall be convicted without the concurrence of two thirds of the members present" (Art. I, §§ 2–3). `[attested: Cornell LII, Art. I]`
- **Israel:**
  - The opposition has called for the Shin Bet chief's immediate dismissal if it forms a government. `[press: Harel]`
  - Keinon reports it could use a speaker of its own to advance:
    - a state commission of inquiry into 7 October;
    - a law on conscripting haredi (ultra-Orthodox) men;
    - the rollback of overhaul legislation. `[analysis: Keinon]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - A successor's moves need the same scrutiny as the predecessor's. The project's planned catalogue of the government's moves is now designed to code each move by its aim as well as its target, and that coding applies to any government.
  - Whether accountability for the previous government reads as accountability or as retaliation is, on the project's terms, a question about aims, settled by stated purpose or an act, not by commentary. `[conjectural]`

**8. A third model: the United Kingdom**

- **The model.** A returning officer certifies the result, and the House of Commons seats the member certified. Disputes go to an election court, not to the House. `[conjectural: a general account; the statute, the Representation of the People Act 1983, was not read here because legislation.gov.uk is blocked from this session]`
- **An example.** Tony Benn, by then Viscount Stansgate, won the Bristol South-East by-election of 1961. Two judges, Gorman and McNair JJ, held that he could not keep the seat after succeeding to his father's peerage, and that the runner-up took it without a new election. `[run: attested, Lexis, Tillman, 2025 Harv. J.L. & Pub. Pol'y Per Curiam 1, n.13]`
- **What the contrast teaches:**
  - In this third arrangement, neither the chamber nor a security service decides who sits. An official certifies and a court decides disputes.
  - In Israel, the official who certifies is a Supreme Court justice, Noam Sohlberg. That puts Israel nearer this model than the American one. In this model the certifying official and a court, not a security service, settle disputes. That is why Sohlberg's instruction to the Shin Bet chief to investigate matters. `[conjectural]`

## What the juxtaposition means for the project

- **The model conjecture gains a test it lacked.** The conjecture holds that in Israel what the coalition can do and where the system is open to attack are the same fact. If the 26th Knesset elects a speaker against the outgoing government, the two come apart. The part of the threat that comes from the system's standing design would then prove to change with elections rather than to stand fixed. `[conjectural]`
- **The catalogue's gaps recur in both systems.** "Contesting a certified result" is a mechanism in both. The executive's use of law enforcement or security services is an instrument in both. A chamber judging its own members is a constraint the US has and the project's catalogue of threats (its list of actors, instruments, values and checks) does not record for Israel. All three are listed as candidates, not added.
- **Compliance is the floor in both.** Rows 4 and 6 end at the same place: courts and constitutions bind only as far as the executive complies. That is where the project's catalogue already places non-compliance, reached only by extra-legal pressure.
