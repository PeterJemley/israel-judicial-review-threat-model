# Three constitutional settlements: Britain, the United States and Israel

**What this is.** A research note comparing three ways of settling who has the last word on a constitution, and asking what the comparison suggests for the project. It is exploratory: written after the project's data were seen, so nothing in it counts as a planned test. Nothing here is applied to the main note. It was written at the project author's direction on 10 November 2026.

**Author.** Peter Jemley, who maintains this project.

**What "the project" is.** This repository tests a sentence in Barak Medina's 2026 article in the *International Journal of Constitutional Law* (I·CON), p. 685: that the 2023 judicial plan posed "an imminent risk to Israel's existence as a democracy". It reads the claim through the threat-assessment formula Threat = Capability × Intent: what an actor can do, multiplied by what it means to do. The main document is [the note](../medina-threat-conjecture-note.md); the [README](../README.md) is the plain-language entry point. Project labels are explained where they appear.

## 1. Three settlements, on two questions

### How hard is it to change the constitutional rules?

- **Britain: by ordinary statute.**
  - Sumption says any constitutional rule can be made, changed or repealed by an ordinary Act of Parliament. His examples are devolution and the rules for dissolving Parliament. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
  - Sumption calls the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 Britain's one attempt to entrench a constitutional provision. A one-line Act, passed by a simple majority, got round it. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
  - Bogdanor draws the opposite lesson from the same Act: fundamental change was brought in quickly, without proper scrutiny, by a makeshift majority. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 24:36; paraphrased]`
- **The United States: by supermajorities.**
  - Bickel says the amending clause "incorporates an extreme minority veto" (p. 21), so it is rarely a practical check on the Court. `[summary: Bickel, p. 21]`
  - Sumption gives *Citizens United* as his example. Once the Court held limits on corporate election spending unconstitutional, the rule could change only if the Court changed its mind, or by an amendment passed by two thirds of each house of Congress and three quarters of the state legislatures. He says this rigidity favours powerful vested interests, and points to the repeated failure of attempts to introduce gun control. The transcript is unclear on his exact word for what it hands those interests. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
- **Israel: mostly by a simple majority.**
  - Most Basic Laws can be amended by a simple majority of the members voting. `[attested: Medina, p. 708, as the note gives it]`
  - The project's list of every law titled Basic Law has 130 laws, passed from February 1958 to July 2026. Thirty-four of them were passed in the 1990s. Of the 130, 14 were challenged in the Supreme Court. `[attested: research/frame/basic-law-frame.csv, counted on 10 November 2026]`

### Who guards against abuse by a majority?

- **Sumption: politics.**
  - The political process, by making room for divisions of interest and opinion, is in his view far more effective than the law at avoiding majority tyranny. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
  - Weighing a right against a public interest, such as privacy against the work of the intelligence services, is a choice between incommensurable aims. In his view such choices belong to politics, not to judges. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
- **Bogdanor: judges, once consensus fails.**
  - The consensus and conventions that Sumption's view relies on have broken down. `[video: Inner Temple, chapters 3:50 and 24:36; paraphrased]`
  - Judges are needed for the rights of small and unpopular minorities, such as asylum seekers, prisoners and suspected terrorists, especially in periods of moral panic. `[video: Inner Temple, chapters 24:36 and 40:02; paraphrased]`
- **Bickel: courts, with restraint.**
  - He quotes Thayer's warning that judicial review tends to "dwarf the political capacity of the people, and to deaden its sense of moral responsibility" (pp. 21–22). That is Sumption's worry, in an American voice. `[summary: Bickel, pp. 21–22]` `[conjectural]` for the link.
  - He quotes Learned Hand: "a society so riven that the spirit of moderation is gone, no court *can* save" (p. 23). `[summary: Bickel, p. 23]`
  - He still justifies review by what courts do well: they work out what "enduring values" require, with "the leisure, the training, and the insulation" of scholars (pp. 24–26). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 24–26]`
  - Courts husband that power by often declining to decide. In his formula the Court "checks, it legitimates, or it does neither" (p. 200). `[summary: Bickel, p. 200]`
- **Israel: factions inside the coalition, then the Court.**
  - Rosen-Zvi and Yarom write: "For many years, this constitutional 'time bomb' remained dormant due to internal divisions within governing coalitions. Factions served as a check on one another, preventing Basic Law amendments for self-serving, rights-infringing, or otherwise unconstitutional purposes." `[attested: reply, p. 712]`
  - They add that "this safeguard has weakened considerably over the past decade and was effectively eliminated in the most recent elections". `[attested: reply, p. 712]`
  - That is Sumption's safeguard, political accommodation, working inside a single coalition. `[conjectural]`
  - **The sliding scale.** Medina describes the Court's doctrine on Basic Law amendments as a sliding scale: the further an amendment departs from the unwritten constitution, the broader and more lasting the support it needs. `[attested: the note's Background, citing Medina, pp. 702, 708]` On this reading, the scale makes that safeguard a legal requirement. `[conjectural]`

## 2. Where Israel sits

- **A hybrid.** Israel combines Britain's ease of amendment with a court that reviews Basic Laws:
  - since *Mizrahi Bank* (1995), the Court has held the Basic Laws binding on the Knesset's ordinary legislation;
  - since January 2024, by 12 of 15 justices, it has held that it may review the Basic Laws themselves in extreme cases.

  `[attested: the note's Background; docket row 9]`
- **Bogdanor's mentions of Israel.** He names Israel as one of the few democracies without a codified constitution, and as moving towards one through its Basic Laws. He also says Israel provides for referendums in a Basic Law. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 3:50; paraphrased]`
- **Bickel's American structure is absent.** Several of Bickel's arguments rest on it: an executive elected separately from the legislature, a federation and a constitution very hard to amend. `[summary: Bridges, "First test"]`
  - In Israel, coalition discipline "generally ensures that the executive and legislative branches – the government and the Knesset, respectively – are on the same page". `[analysis: Herb Keinon, Jerusalem Post, 1 November 2026, as quoted in research/2026-11-05-us-israel-juxtaposition.md, row 3]`
  - Bickel writes: "In an enforcement crisis of any real proportions, the judiciary is wholly dependent upon the Executive" (p. 252). `[summary: Bickel, p. 252]`
  - In Israel, that executive is often the coalition whose measure the Court ruled against. `[summary: Bridges, "First test"]` `[conjectural]`

## 3. Candidate points for the project

None of these is applied, and none is tested. Each says which part of the project it would touch.

1. **Three options, not two.**
   - Bickel's Court "checks, it legitimates, or it does neither" (p. 200). Upholding a law is itself an intervention, because the Court "can generate consent and may impart permanence" (p. 129). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 129, 200]`
   - T1 is the project's test of the warning signs Medina reports, scored from legislative records by coders not given the judgments. Its outcome class "Adverse" holds a strike-down, a deferral, two warnings and two laws upheld but read narrowly. `[attested: docket rows 4 to 9]` The rows are those of the project's codebook, its table of enactments the Supreme Court has ruled on, most of them Basic Laws ([codebook/m4-codebook.csv](../codebook/m4-codebook.csv)). The docket is its record of each ruling ([coding-2026-10-22/docket.json](coding-2026-10-22/docket.json)).
   - Opened as issue 40, to code the remedy beside the outcome.
2. **Rejecting a principle is not defying an order.**
   - Lincoln said of *Dred Scott*, "We offer no *resistance* to it", yet "we nevertheless do oppose that decision as a political rule" (p. 260). Bickel adds: "For enforcement is a crisis of the system, not its norm" (p. 266). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 260, 266]`
   - The project's ontology treats "non-compliance with rulings" as one instrument. `[attested: the note, Appendix 1]`
   - Opened as issue 41, to split it in two.
3. **Purpose, not motive.**
   - "Legislative motives are nearly always mixed and nearly never professed" (p. 214). Bickel distinguishes motive from "purpose", which is either "the name given to the Court's objective assessment of the effect of a statute" or "a conclusionary term denoting the Court's independent judgment of the constitutionally allowable end that the legislature could have had in view" (p. 209). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 209, 214]`
   - This fits two of the project's rules:
     - the `situated_beneficiary` sign is coded from a closed list of facts dated no later than passage, not from stated motive;
     - T2, the catalogue of the government's moves, takes a move's aim only "from a stated purpose or an act that only that aim explains".

     `[attested: the note, T1's step 3 and change log (be)]`
   - In the Incapacity case, Justice Willner held that generality concerns how the norm applies, not the personal considerations of the members who voted for it. `[attested: research/2026-11-05-court-signs.md, citing her para. 2]`
4. **The people win in the end.**
   - Bickel asks: "Having been checked, should the people persist; having been educated, should the people insist, must they not win over every fundamental principle save one—which is the principle that they must win?" (p. 28). He also writes that "on the supreme occasion, when the system is forced to find ultimate self-consistency, the principle of self-rule must prevail" (p. 261). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 28, 261]`
   - Medina's rejoinder concedes the same of his own model: the constraint of constitutional identity "will lose its efficacy" if a broad political consensus abandons liberal democratic values. `[attested: the note, Prior art]`
   - The project already records this. Its doctrine matrix (M0) sets out the sliding scale's predictions. Its note on the published exchange records the reply's objection O11: the scale would require the Court to uphold a measure restricting minority rights if an overwhelming majority backed it. `[attested: research/2026-11-09-reply-and-rejoinder.md]` Bickel supplies an older statement of the same point. `[conjectural]`
5. **Coalition pluralism as a constraint that changes with time.**
   - The reply's "time bomb" passage (section 1 of this note) suggests a candidate row for the matrix of constraints against instruments (M3): political accommodation inside the governing coalition. `[conjectural]`
   - It bears on the model conjecture (C-model). C-model holds that the coalition's capability and the system's vulnerability are one fact. On this reading, that holds only once pluralism inside the coalition is gone. `[conjectural]`
   - It also bears on expectation E1 in the US–Israel comparison: whether the new Knesset elects a speaker from outside the outgoing coalition. The speaker set the vote for "next week on Tuesday", which that note reads as 17 November 2026. `[press: Jerusalem Post, 10 November 2026, as recorded in research/2026-11-05-us-israel-juxtaposition.md]`
   - The reply's "over the past decade" matches the split at about 2015 in matrix M1, the project's table of the coalition's capability and intent over time. `[conjectural]`
6. **Frequent amendment.**
   - "The familiarity of amendment will breed a species of contempt and incapacitate the document for symbolic service" (p. 105). `[summary: Bickel, p. 105]`
   - The 34 laws titled Basic Law passed in the 1990s give that worry numbers, without any new test. `[conjectural]`
7. **Sumption as a scholarly form of rival R2.**
   - R2 is the rival explanation that the plan was a good-faith rebalancing of an activist court. The note's table of rivals gives it no scholarly source, while R4, populist concentration of power, cites Roznai and Cohen. `[attested: the note, Rivals]`
   - Sumption argues that politics, not courts, should guard against abuse by a majority. He doubts that rules should be entrenched behind a special procedure such as a supermajority, and holds that judges should not weigh rights against public interests. `[video: Inner Temple, chapters 12:04 and 40:02; paraphrased]`
   - That is the strongest scholarly form of the view R2 attributes to the plan's supporters. It sits uneasily with Medina's sliding scale, which asks judges to require broader support for larger departures. Sumption said nothing about Israel or the 2023 plan. `[conjectural]`
8. **Judicial appointments.**
   - Oliver asked whether giving courts a constitution to enforce would politicize appointments. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 24:36; paraphrased]`
   - Sumption said political involvement in appointing judges inevitably politicizes them: American elections have become partly a contest for the right to appoint Supreme Court justices. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
   - Bogdanor answered conditionally: if, as he thought Sumption did, one believes the law intrudes too far into politics, there is something to be said for the American method, because it makes the interpretation of rights follow the democratic process after a long time lag. He added that Britain would not want to go that far. What it needs is closer understanding between judges and politicians. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
   - Bickel's version: "To the extent that they are instruments of decisive change, Justices are time bombs, not warheads that explode on impact" (p. 31). He notes exceptions where appointments changed doctrine quickly, Grant's two in 1870 and Justice Black's in 1937 (pp. 31–32). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 31–32]`
   - The 2025 Judicial Selection amendment removes the Bar Association's two members from the committee that selects judges, and adds lawyers nominated by the coalition and by the opposition. `[attested: research/frame/challenges.json]` By its text it took effect when the 26th Knesset first met, on 10 November 2026. No ruling or interim order was found in the press reports and searches read for the project's check-in that day; the Court's docket was not searched. `[press and search-lead, as recorded in research/2026-11-05-us-israel-juxtaposition.md, row 5]`
9. **The liberal elite, from inside.**
   - Bogdanor closed by saying the problems had arisen because the liberal elite, himself included, are bad losers. He said this of the 2016 referendum. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
   - The note's Claim C ("not elite interest") has two halves. At p. 685, Medina says the plan's threats are not to "private interests or ideologies of the liberal elite". At p. 684, he defends the Court's doctrine against the critique that it preserves the hegemony of a liberal elite. `[attested: the note, "The question"]`
   - Bogdanor's remark is a member of a liberal elite describing it from inside, about a vote it lost. It is recorded, not tested. `[conjectural]`
10. **Temporary provisions.**
    - Sumption and Bogdanor read the Fixed-term Parliaments Act in opposite ways. For Sumption, its quick undoing by a one-line Act shows the value of flexibility. For Bogdanor, it illustrates how fast a makeshift majority can bring in fundamental change (section 1 of this note). `[video: Inner Temple, chapters 12:04 and 24:36; paraphrased]`
    - Israel's temporary provisions to Basic Laws changed constitutional rules for a limited time, by an ordinary majority, to meet the needs of the moment. Two of them were the subjects of the Court's two warnings of invalidity (codebook rows 5 and 6, the 2016 budget law and the 2020 amendment in *Shafir*). `[attested: docket rows 5 and 6]`
    - The parallel is this note's: in both countries a constitutional rule was changed or set aside by an ordinary majority for a passing need. `[conjectural]`
