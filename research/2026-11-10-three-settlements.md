# Three constitutional settlements: Britain, the United States and Israel

**What this is.** A research note comparing three ways of settling who has the last word on a constitution, and asking what the comparison suggests for the project. It is exploratory: written after the project's data were seen, so nothing in it counts as a planned test. Nothing here is applied to the main note. It was written at the project author's direction on 10 November 2026.

**Author.** Peter Jemley, who maintains this project.

**What "the project" is.** This repository tests a sentence in Barak Medina's 2026 article in the *International Journal of Constitutional Law* (I·CON), p. 685: that the 2023 judicial plan posed "an imminent risk to Israel's existence as a democracy". It reads the claim through the threat-assessment formula Threat = Capability × Intent: what an actor can do, multiplied by what it means to do. The main document is [the note](../medina-threat-conjecture-note.md); the [README](../README.md) is the plain-language entry point. Project labels are explained where they appear.

## 1. Three settlements, on two questions

### How hard is it to change the constitutional rules?

- **Britain: by ordinary statute.**
  - Sumption says any constitutional rule can be made, changed or repealed by an ordinary Act of Parliament. His examples are devolution and the rules for dissolving Parliament. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
  - Britain's one attempt to entrench a rule was the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011. A one-line Act, passed by a simple majority, got round it. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
  - Bogdanor draws the opposite lesson from the same Act: fundamental change was brought in quickly, without proper scrutiny, by a makeshift majority. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 24:36; paraphrased]`
- **The United States: by supermajorities.**
  - Bickel says the amending clause "incorporates an extreme minority veto" (p. 21), so it is rarely a practical check on the Court. `[summary: Bickel, p. 21]`
  - Sumption gives *Citizens United* as his example. Once the Court held limits on corporate election spending unconstitutional, the rule could change only if the Court changed its mind, or by an amendment passed by two thirds of each house of Congress and three quarters of the state legislatures. He says this rigidity hands a blocking power to vested interests, and points to the failures of gun control. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
- **Israel: mostly by a simple majority.**
  - Most Basic Laws can be amended by a simple majority of the members voting. `[attested: Medina, p. 708, as the note gives it]`
  - The project's list of every law titled Basic Law has 130 laws passed from 1958 to 2026. Thirty-four of them were passed in the 1990s, and 14 were challenged in the High Court. `[attested: research/frame/basic-law-frame.csv, counted on 10 November 2026]`

### Who guards against abuse by a majority?

- **Sumption: politics.**
  - The political process, by making room for divisions of interest and opinion, is in his view far more effective than the law at avoiding majority tyranny. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 12:04; paraphrased]`
  - Weighing a right against a public interest, such as privacy against the work of the intelligence services, is a choice between incommensurable aims. In his view such choices belong to politics, not to judges. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
- **Bogdanor: judges, once consensus fails.**
  - The consensus and conventions that Sumption's view relies on have broken down. `[video: Inner Temple, chapters 3:50 and 24:36; paraphrased]`
  - Judges are needed for the rights of small and unpopular minorities, such as asylum seekers, prisoners and suspected terrorists, especially in periods of moral panic. `[video: Inner Temple, chapters 24:36 and 40:02; paraphrased]`
- **Bickel: courts, with restraint.**
  - He quotes Thayer's warning that judicial review tends to "dwarf the political capacity of the people, and to deaden its sense of moral responsibility" (pp. 21–22). That is Sumption's worry, in an American voice. `[summary: Bickel, pp. 21–22]` `[conjectural]` for the link.
  - He quotes Learned Hand: "a society so riven that the spirit of moderation is gone, no court *can* save" (p. 23). `[summary: Bickel, p. 23]`
  - He still justifies review by what courts do well: they work out what "enduring values" require, with "the leisure, the training, and the insulation" of scholars (pp. 24–26). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 24–26]`
  - Courts husband that power by often declining to decide. In his formula the Court "checks, it legitimates, or it does neither" (p. 200). `[summary: Bickel, p. 200]`
- **Israel: factions inside the coalition, then the Court.**
  - Rosen-Zvi and Yarom write: "For many years, this constitutional 'time bomb' remained dormant due to internal divisions within governing coalitions. Factions served as a check on one another." `[attested: reply, p. 712]`
  - They add that "this safeguard has weakened considerably over the past decade and was effectively eliminated in the most recent elections". `[attested: reply, p. 712]`
  - That is Sumption's safeguard, political accommodation, working inside a single coalition. `[conjectural]`
  - **The sliding scale.** Medina's account of the Court's doctrine is that the more an amendment departs from Israel's unwritten constitution, the broader the support it needs. On this reading, it makes that safeguard a legal requirement. `[conjectural]`

## 2. Where Israel sits

- **A hybrid.** Israel combines Britain's ease of amendment with a court that reviews Basic Laws:
  - since *Mizrahi Bank* (1995), the Court has held the Basic Laws binding on the Knesset;
  - since January 2024, by 12 of 15 justices, it has held that it may review the Basic Laws themselves in extreme cases.

  `[attested: the note's Background; docket row 9]`
- **Bogdanor's mentions of Israel.** He names Israel as one of the few democracies without a codified constitution, and as moving towards one through its Basic Laws. He also says Israel provides for referendums in a Basic Law. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 3:50; paraphrased]`
- **Bickel's American structure is absent.** Several of Bickel's arguments rest on it: an executive elected separately from the legislature, a federation and a constitution very hard to amend.
  - Israel's government holds office with the confidence of a single-chamber Knesset, so the legislative and executive majorities are normally one coalition. `[summary: Bridges, "First test"]`
  - It follows that the executive on which, in Bickel's words, "the judiciary is wholly dependent" for enforcement (p. 252) is normally the coalition whose measure the Court ruled against. `[summary: Bickel, p. 252]` `[conjectural]` for the inference.

## 3. Candidate points for the project

None of these is applied, and none is tested. Each says which part of the project it would touch.

1. **Three options, not two.**
   - Bickel's Court "checks, it legitimates, or it does neither" (p. 200). Upholding a law is itself an intervention, because the Court "can generate consent and may impart permanence" (p. 129). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 129, 200]`
   - T1 is the project's blind test of the warning signs Medina reports. Its outcome class "Adverse" holds a strike-down, a deferral, two warnings and two laws upheld but read narrowly. `[attested: docket rows 4 to 9]`
   - Opened as issue 40, to code the remedy beside the outcome.
2. **Rejecting a principle is not defying an order.**
   - Lincoln said of *Dred Scott*, "We offer no *resistance* to it", yet "we nevertheless do oppose that decision as a political rule" (p. 260). Bickel adds: "For enforcement is a crisis of the system, not its norm" (p. 266). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 260, 266]`
   - The project's ontology treats "non-compliance with rulings" as one instrument. `[attested: the note, Appendix 1]`
   - Opened as issue 41, to split it in two.
3. **Purpose, not motive.**
   - "Legislative motives are nearly always mixed and nearly never professed" (p. 214). Bickel accepts "purpose" as "the name given to the Court's objective assessment of the effect of a statute" (p. 209). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 209, 214]`
   - This fits two of the project's rules:
     - the `situated_beneficiary` sign is coded from a closed list of facts dated no later than passage, not from stated motive;
     - T2, the catalogue of the government's moves, takes a move's aim only "from a stated purpose or an act that only that aim explains".

     `[attested: the note, T1's step 3 and change log (be)]`
   - In the Incapacity case, Justice Willner held that generality concerns how the norm applies, not the personal considerations of the members who voted for it. `[attested: research/2026-11-05-court-signs.md, citing her para. 2]`
4. **The people win in the end.**
   - Bickel asks: "Having been checked, should the people persist; having been educated, should the people insist, must they not win over every fundamental principle save one—which is the principle that they must win?" (p. 28). He also writes that "on the supreme occasion, when the system is forced to find ultimate self-consistency, the principle of self-rule must prevail" (p. 261). `[summary: Bickel, pp. 28, 261]`
   - Medina's rejoinder concedes the same of his own model: the constraint of constitutional identity "will lose its efficacy" if a broad political consensus abandons liberal democratic values. `[attested: the note, Prior art]`
   - The project's doctrine matrix (M0) and its record of the reply's objection O11 already carry this. Bickel supplies its oldest statement. `[conjectural]`
5. **Coalition pluralism as a constraint that changes with time.**
   - The reply's "time bomb" passage (section 1) suggests a candidate row for the matrix of constraints against instruments (M3): political accommodation inside the governing coalition. `[conjectural]`
   - It bears on the model conjecture (C-model). C-model holds that the coalition's capability and the system's vulnerability are one fact. On this reading, that holds only once pluralism inside the coalition is gone. `[conjectural]`
   - It also bears on expectation E1 in the US–Israel comparison: whether the new Knesset elects a speaker from outside the outgoing coalition. The vote is set for 17 November 2026. `[attested: research/2026-11-05-us-israel-juxtaposition.md]`
   - The reply's "over the past decade" matches matrix M1's split at about 2015. `[conjectural]`
6. **Frequent amendment.**
   - "The familiarity of amendment will breed a species of contempt and incapacitate the document for symbolic service" (p. 105). `[summary: Bickel, p. 105]`
   - The 34 laws titled Basic Law passed in the 1990s give that worry numbers, without any new test. `[conjectural]`
7. **Sumption as a scholarly form of rival R2.**
   - R2 is the rival explanation that the plan was a good-faith rebalancing of an activist court. The note's table of rivals gives it no scholarly source, while R4 cites Roznai and Cohen. `[attested: the note, Rivals]`
   - Sumption would accept the safeguard that Medina's sliding scale demands, broad political support. He would object to judges enforcing it. `[conjectural, from chapters 12:04 and 40:02]`
8. **Judicial appointments.**
   - Oliver asked whether giving courts a constitution to enforce would politicize appointments. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 24:36; paraphrased]`
   - Sumption said political involvement in appointing judges inevitably politicizes them: American elections have become partly a contest for the right to appoint Supreme Court justices. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
   - Bogdanor said the American method makes the interpretation of rights follow the democratic process, after a time lag. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
   - Bickel's version is that justices "are time bombs, not warheads that explode on impact" (p. 31). `[summary: Bickel, p. 31]`
   - The 2025 Judicial Selection amendment removes the Bar Association's two members from the committee that selects judges, and adds lawyers nominated by the coalition and by the opposition. `[attested: research/frame/challenges.json]` By its text it took effect when the 26th Knesset first met, on 10 November 2026; no ruling on the petitions against it was found. `[attested: research/2026-11-05-us-israel-juxtaposition.md, row 5]`
9. **The liberal elite, from inside.**
   - Bogdanor closed by saying the problems had arisen because the liberal elite, himself included, are bad losers. He said this of the 2016 referendum. `[video: Inner Temple, chapter 40:02; paraphrased]`
   - The elite critique that Medina answers (Claim C in the note) is here voiced from inside the elite, about a vote the elite lost. It is recorded, not tested. `[conjectural]`
10. **Temporary provisions.**
    - Sumption and Bogdanor read the Fixed-term Parliaments Act in opposite ways: as proof of flexibility, and as proof of how fast a makeshift majority can make fundamental change (section 1).
    - Israel's temporary provisions to Basic Laws were the subjects of the Court's two warnings (codebook rows 5 and 6). `[attested: docket rows 5 and 6]`
    - The parallel is this note's. `[conjectural]`
