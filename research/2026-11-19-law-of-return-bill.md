# A bill to amend the Law of Return (November 2026), set against the project

**What this is.** A research note on a bill submitted to the new Knesset that would change who counts as a Jew under Israel's Law of Return. It records what the bill would do, the court ruling it targets, the earlier attempts, and where it touches the project. Nothing here is applied to the main note, and no coded cell, number or verdict changes. It was written at the project author's direction on 19 November 2026.

**Author.** Peter Jemley, who maintains this project.

**What "the project" is.** This repository tests a sentence in Barak Medina's 2026 article in the *International Journal of Constitutional Law* (I·CON), p. 685: that the 2023 judicial plan posed "an imminent risk to Israel's existence as a democracy". It reads the claim through the threat-assessment formula Threat = Capability × Intent: what an actor can do, multiplied by what it means to do. The main document is [the note](../medina-threat-conjecture-note.md); the [README](../README.md) is the plain-language entry point. Project labels are explained where they appear.

## 1. What the bill would do

- **Who submitted it, and when.** Otzma Yehudit, a party of the outgoing coalition, submitted the bill two days after the 26th Knesset was sworn in. Its sponsor is MK Limor Son Har-Melech. `[press: JPost, 17 November]` The note describes Otzma Yehudit as "a far-right coalition party". `[attested: the note, matrix M1, 2026 row]` The Knesset was sworn in on 10 November 2026. `[attested: research/2026-11-05-harel-evaluation.md, addendum of 10 November]`
- **The change.** The Law of Return defines a Jew as someone born to a Jewish mother who is not a member of another religion, or someone who has converted. The bill adds that the conversion must be "according to halacha", meaning Jewish religious law. `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **Its stated aim.** The report says the aim is "to render meaningless" the High Court ruling that recognized Reform and Conservative conversions "for the purposes of aliyah and registration". `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **What it leaves open.** The bill does not define "according to halacha", or say who would decide whether a conversion meets it. `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
  - The report says that if the term is read as referring only to conversions the Chief Rabbinate approves, private Orthodox conversions it does not recognize "could also be affected".
  - It adds the reverse: the term's ambiguity "could allow the courts to interpret it as referring to the religious law of any denomination of Judaism".

  `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **Conversions abroad.** The bill makes no distinction between conversions performed in Israel and abroad. On an analysis by the Israel Democracy Institute, as the report gives it, Reform and Conservative conversions performed overseas "would no longer confer eligibility for aliyah as Jews". `[press: JPost, 17 November, citing the Israel Democracy Institute]`
- **What it does not change.** The bill does not repeal the "grandchild clause". Someone with a Jewish grandparent could still immigrate as a family member, but would not be registered as Jewish. `[press: JPost, 17 November]`

## 2. The ruling it targets

- **The 2021 ruling.** In 2021 an expanded panel of nine justices ruled that the state must recognize Reform and Conservative conversions performed in Israel, within a recognized Jewish community, for the purposes of the Law of Return. The court "emphasized that the issue was civil, not religious". The report calls this the Dahan ruling. `[press: JPost, 17 November]` The judgment was not read for this note.
- **Room for legislation.** "Former Supreme Court president Esther Hayut explicitly wrote that the Knesset was entitled to give the term 'conversion' a different meaning through future legislation." `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **Earlier recognition.** The High Court recognized Reform and Conservative conversions performed abroad for population-registry purposes in 1986 and 1987. That recognition was later extended to the Law of Return. `[press: JPost, 17 November]`

## 3. Earlier attempts

- **1970.** The Knesset first defined a Jew in the Law of Return in 1970, as someone "born to a Jewish mother or who has converted". The report says lawmakers deliberately left out "according to halacha", as a compromise to avoid a rupture with Diaspora Jewry. `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **The 25th Knesset (2022–2026).** The Knesset's records hold four private bills titled "Law of Return (Amendment – adding the word 'according to halacha')" (this note's rendering of the Hebrew). Their sponsors were:
  - Simcha Rothman (Religious Zionism);
  - Avi Maoz (Noam);
  - Meir Porush (United Torah Judaism);
  - Avichai Boaron (Likud).

  All four were laid on the table for a preliminary reading, and none went further. `[attested: Knesset open data, bills 2235343, 2237643, 2241047 and 2242921, their sponsors and factions]`
- **Fifty years of resistance.** The report says "prime ministers from both the Right and Left have resisted repeated attempts to add those words for more than 50 years". `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **Netanyahu's block.** The report says that "As recently as the final days of the 25th Knesset, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu blocked an identical proposal by MK Simcha Rothman, partly because of concerns over the response from Diaspora Jewry." `[press: JPost, 17 November]`

## 4. Why it matters beyond Israel

- **Who is affected.** The report cites a Pew Research Center survey: 37% of Jewish adults in the United States identify as Reform, 17% as Conservative and 9% as Orthodox. `[press: JPost, 17 November, citing Pew]`
- **Warnings.** The Jewish Agency and Jewish organizations abroad have warned against similar proposals before, fearing "a deep rift with world Jewry". `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **Numbers in Israel.** The report says the 2021 ruling affected about 326 Reform and Conservative converts a year in Israel. It cites an Israel Democracy Institute survey in which 46% of Israeli Jews recognize people with non-Orthodox conversions as Jewish, and 39% do not. `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
- **The supporters' case.**
  - Supporters say the bill "addresses a fundamental issue that the Knesset has avoided for decades".
  - They say it is intended to prevent the state from recognizing as Jewish people who cannot marry or be buried as Jews under the Chief Rabbinate's rules.
  - MK Simcha Rothman has argued that the intention is not to harm liberal Judaism but to prevent an influx of "fictitious conversions".

  `[press: JPost, 17 November]`

## 5. Where it touches the project

None of these points is applied, and none is tested.

- **Outside the main test.** The Law of Return is an ordinary law, not a Basic Law. So the bill falls outside T1, the project's test of how the Supreme Court rules on Basic Laws, and outside the codebook, the project's table of the laws that test covers. `[attested: the note, T1; codebook/README.md]`
- **A move on religious norms.** One of the project's rival explanations, R3, is Medina's own account: the 2023 plan aimed to transform Israel's constitutional identity. In the project's table of rivals, R3 predicts follow-on moves on religious norms, among others, citing Medina at p. 685. `[attested: the note, Rivals]`
  - A bill restricting which conversions the state recognizes might count as such a move. `[conjectural]`
  - But it is a private bill from one party of the outgoing coalition, not a government act. Netanyahu blocked an identical bill in the last Knesset (section 3).
  - The campaign to add these words is also more than fifty years old (section 3). That weighs against reading the bill as a product of the 2023 plan in particular. `[conjectural]`
  - So it is a candidate for T2, the project's catalogue of the government's moves, and not yet a coded entry. `[conjectural]`
- **Answering a ruling by legislation.** The bill would answer a High Court ruling with an ordinary statute, the instrument the project's catalogue calls a "regular statute" (the note, Appendix 1).
  - As the report gives it, Hayut wrote that the Knesset was entitled to give "conversion" a different meaning through legislation. The bill's sponsors present it as "a legitimate decision by the sovereign legislature". `[press: JPost, 17 November]`
  - So it is not non-compliance with a ruling, the instrument issue 41 proposes to split, because it would change the law the ruling applied. `[conjectural]`
  - It is closer to what Alexander Bickel, in his account of Lincoln and *Dred Scott*, treats as lawful: opposing a decision's principle through the elected branches ([2026-11-10-three-settlements.md](2026-11-10-three-settlements.md)). Here, though, the ruling itself left that route open. `[conjectural]`
- **The published reply to Medina.** Rosen-Zvi and Yarom name the Law of Return among the ordinary laws that offer "crucial insight into the kind of state Israel is". They say such laws "cannot be ignored in reconstructing the content of its constitutional law" (I·CON, pp. 717–718). `[attested: research/2026-11-09-reply-and-rejoinder.md, objection O4]`
  - The bill would change who the state counts as Jewish under that law, so it bears on their claim. `[conjectural]`
- **Its chances.** On 17 November, Otzma Yehudit's seven members voted for Likud's candidate for speaker. He lost 52 to 66 to the candidate of the bloc opposed to the outgoing coalition, led by Gadi Eisenkot. `[attested: Knesset open data, plenum votes 46730 and 46731; research/2026-11-05-us-israel-juxtaposition.md, E1]` The bill's chances cannot be read from that vote, since Likud's leader blocked an identical bill in the last Knesset (section 3). `[conjectural]`

## 6. What to watch

- Whether the bill appears in the Knesset's records, and whether it is brought to a preliminary reading.
- Whether any party in the bloc now leading the Knesset, or any party a new government would need, supports it.
