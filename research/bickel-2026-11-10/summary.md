## The argument in brief

Bickel starts from what he calls the "counter-majoritarian difficulty." When the Supreme Court strikes down a statute, it overrides the elected representatives of the people "of the here and now" (p. 17), and the constitutional text does not clearly give it that power (pp. 1–14). He justifies judicial review anyway. Governments serve both immediate needs and "enduring values," and courts are better placed than legislatures to work out what those values require, with "the leisure, the training, and the insulation" of scholars (pp. 24–26). But he admits this does not fully reconcile review with democracy (p. 27).

His answer is that the Court has three options, not two: it "checks, it legitimates, or it does neither" (p. 200). When it strikes a law down, it must act on principle. When it upholds a law, that is also an intervention, because it lends the law the Court's prestige and can make it permanent (pp. 129–131). The third option, doing neither, is the key to the whole book. Standing, ripeness, certiorari, the political question doctrine, vagueness, delegation, desuetude, narrow statutory construction and timing let the Court avoid both striking down and blessing. They leave room for expedient compromise without abandoning principle, and they open a "colloquy" with the elected branches and the public (pp. 69–72, 111–198). These choices are governed by prudence, not principle, though never by whim (p. 133).

Bickel models this on Lincoln. Lincoln held to the principle that slavery was wrong while tolerating compromises with its existence, and he obeyed the *Dred Scott* judgment while refusing to accept it "as a political rule" (pp. 65–68, 259–261). In Bickel's account, the Court's law ultimately prevails only if it wins assent. The Court "labors under the obligation to succeed" (p. 239) and is "a court of last resort presumptively only" (p. 258).

## Key terms

- **Counter-majoritarian difficulty** (pp. 16–23). Judicial review "thwarts the will of representatives of the actual people of the here and now" (p. 17). Democracy means that a representative majority can reverse a decision, and "This power is of the essence" (p. 17). Review is therefore "a deviant institution in the American democracy" (p. 18).
- **Enduring values / principle** (pp. 24–28, 199). Government actions have immediate practical effects and also a "bearing on values we hold to have more general and permanent interest." Those values "must be continually derived, enunciated, and seen in relevant application" (p. 24). Principles are "general propositions," as Holmes called them, and "organizing ideas of universal validity in the given universe of a culture and a place." Bickel calls such terms "evocative, not definitional" (p. 199).
- **The triune function** (pp. 69–70, 200). The Court strikes down, legitimates, or does neither. "It may do neither, and therein lies the secret of its ability to maintain itself in the tension between principle and expediency" (p. 69).
- **Legitimation** (pp. 29–31, 129–131). Upholding a law is not neutral. The Court "can generate consent and may impart permanence" (p. 129). This is a point taken from Charles Black's *The People and the Court* and turned around.
- **Passive virtues** (Ch. 4; pp. 200–201). Techniques for "not doing": standing, case or controversy, no advisory opinions, ripeness, denying certiorari, dismissing appeals, the political question doctrine, vagueness, delegation, desuetude, narrow statutory construction, narrow procedural holdings, and deferred remedies ("all deliberate speed").
- **Colloquy** (pp. 70–71, 156, 179, 240, 254). A "Socratic colloquy with the other institutions of government and with society as a whole" (pp. 70–71). The Court poses questions to legislatures, trial courts and the public, and the issue "ripens" before final judgment.
- **Lincolnian tension** (pp. 65–72). Principled government sometimes requires holding a principle as an absolute goal while tolerating compromises on the way to it. "Mr. Wechsler's dilemma is a false one" (p. 68).
- **Prudence** (p. 133). "The antithesis of principle in an institution that represents decency and reason is not whim or even expediency, but prudence."
- **"Plessy v. Ferguson's Error"** (pp. 71, 197). Legitimating a compromise "on principle, across the board." Bickel thinks the 1896 Court's mistake was upholding segregation in principle, not failing to strike it down early.
- **Political rule** (pp. 259–264). Lincoln's distinction: obey the specific decree, but remain free to oppose the principle through votes, legislation and relitigation until the Court changes course.
- **Obligation to succeed** (p. 239). "The Court is a leader of opinion, not a mere register of it, but it must lead opinion, not merely impose its own; and—the short of it is—it labors under the obligation to succeed."
- **Teacher / national seminar** (pp. 26, 188). Justices "are inevitably teachers in a vital national seminar" (Rostow, p. 26). The Court is "second only to the presidency in having effectively at its disposal the resources of rhetoric" (p. 188).

## Chapter-by-chapter

### Foreword (Harry H. Wellington, 1986), pp. ix–xii

- Places the book within the Hand–Wechsler–Black debate. Bickel shared Black's functional case for review but found Black's scope dangerous, and found Wechsler's demand for neutral principles too rigid, since it would force the Court to use its power where it could not use it well (p. ix).
- Notes that later scholars such as Choper, Ely and Perry are all in some sense Bickel's students, and that the counter-majoritarian concern now sets the agenda (p. x).
- Argues that the real issue may be the *finality* of constitutional decisions, not judges' lack of accountability. He points to Bickel's Lincolnian view that a ruling is not final "as a political rule" until the elected institutions accept it (p. xii).

### Chapter 1 — Establishment and General Justification of Judicial Review (pp. 1–33)

**Opening.** "The least dangerous branch of the American government is the most extraordinarily powerful court of law the world has ever known" (p. 1). Judicial review is nowhere mentioned in the Constitution: "This is not to say that the power of judicial review cannot be placed in the Constitution; merely that it cannot be found there" (p. 1).

**Marbury v. Madison critiqued (pp. 1–14)**
- Marshall's opinion "begs the question" and "begs the wrong question" (p. 2). The real question is not whether an unconstitutional act can stand but *who* decides that it is unconstitutional (p. 3).
- If legislatures should not set the limits of their own power, neither should courts. That would be "more absurd, because courts are not subject to electoral control" (p. 4).
- Article III's "judicial Power" does not describe what the Court is to do (pp. 5–6). The oath clause binds every officer, so it "is perhaps the strongest textual argument against him" (p. 8). A departmental reading is textually possible, under which each branch would have the final word on the provisions addressed to it (p. 7).
- The Supremacy Clause speaks to *state* judges. Its phrase "made in Pursuance" plausibly means duly enacted (pp. 8–10).
- The strongest case is for federal review of **state** acts, because the federation needs uniform federal law, and the Judiciary Act of 1789 unambiguously provides for it (pp. 10–14). The text "neither supports nor disavows" review of Congress; the principle must be "supposed" (p. 12).

**History (pp. 14–16).** The Framers "foresaw—indeed, invited—it" (p. 15), and a century and a half of settled practice weighs heavily. But history is not enough. Judicial review "is a present instrument of government… we must justify it as a choice in our own time" (p. 16).

**The counter-majoritarian difficulty (pp. 16–23)**
- Hamilton's "people" in *Federalist* 78 is "an abstraction" (p. 16).
- Interest groups do not dissolve the problem, because minorities must still coalesce into majorities to rule (pp. 18–19). The upshot: "judicial review, although not responsible, may have ways of being responsive" (p. 19).
- Agencies and generals differ because their decisions are "interstitial or technical" and "reversible by legislative majorities." So is ordinary judge-made law. Constitutional review is not reversible (pp. 19–20).
- Learned Hand: being ruled by "a bevy of Platonic Guardians" would be "irksome." Consent rests on a "sense of common venture" (p. 20).
- The acquiescence argument fails. The amending clause is an "extreme minority veto," and resignation to the Court's power is not consent to it (p. 21).
- Thayer's warning: review tends to "dwarf the political capacity of the people, and to deaden its sense of moral responsibility" (pp. 21–22). Legislators pass constitutional questions to the courts. Herman Finer observed that the sovereign British Parliament investigates more gently than the constitutionally limited Congress (p. 22).
- Learned Hand, in a speech: "a society so riven that the spirit of moderation is gone, no court *can* save" (p. 23). Judge Gibson, separately, warned that once public opinion is corrupt enough, the dominant party "will laugh at the puny efforts of a dependent power to arrest it in its course" (p. 23).

**The justification from principle (pp. 23–28)**
- Bickel lists criteria the justifying function must meet (p. 24). It must involve policy-making yet differ from legislating, suit the courts' capacities, not get done elsewhere, be acceptable in a democracy, work when needed, and not sap the other branches' sense of responsibility.
- Legislatures under pressure act on expediency. What they lack is "the creative establishment and renewal of a coherent body of principled rules" (p. 25).
- Courts have insulation, scholarly habits, and the concrete case, which is conducive, in a phrase of Holmes, "to thinking things, not words" (pp. 25–26). They offer the opportunity for "the sober second thought" (Stone, p. 26) and act as an educational institution (pp. 26–27).
- He concedes: "No doubt full consistency with democratic theory has not been established" (p. 27). Referring principles to direct referendum would be "the fallacy of the misplaced mystics" (p. 27).
- "Democratic government under law—the slogan pulls in two opposed directions" (p. 27). "Having been checked, should the people persist; having been educated, should the people insist, must they not win over every fundamental principle save one—which is the principle that they must win?" (p. 28).
- His preview of the accommodation: the rule of principle is "seldom rigid," the Court "has ways of persuading before it attempts to coerce," and sustained opinion can nullify its doctrine "directly or by desuetude" (p. 28).

**The mystic function (pp. 29–33)**
- Against Charles Black, legitimation is a "by-product of the checking power," not an independent justification (pp. 29–30).
- Legitimacy comes from good government proven over time and from the consent "of a present majority" (pp. 29–30). Following Hartz's account of the Lockean consensus, judicial review is the fruit of American legitimacy, not its root (p. 30).
- Legitimation can add impetus to measures that a majority adopted only tentatively. That is a reason to stay the Court's hand at times (p. 31).
- The Court personifies the Constitution as a symbol, as the Crown does in Britain (p. 31). The Court is never renewed at a single stroke: "To the extent that they are instruments of decisive change, Justices are time bombs, not warheads that explode on impact" (p. 31). Bickel notes exceptions, such as Grant's two appointments in 1870 and Justice Black's arrival in 1937 (pp. 31–32). Their long, overlapping careers embody continuity (pp. 31–33).
- Review of state acts still needs this justification. The Court "represents the national will against local particularism," but not through electoral responsibility as Congress does (p. 33).

### Chapter 2 — The Premise of Distrust and Rules of Limitation (pp. 34–72)

Justifying judicial review and defining its scope "determine each other" (p. 34). Bickel examines three proposed limiting rules and then offers his own way out.

**Thayer's rule of the clear mistake (pp. 35–46)**
- Thayer (1893): strike down a statute only when the legislature has made a mistake "so clear that it is not open to rational question." The Constitution leaves open "a range of choice and judgment," so that "whatever choice is rational is constitutional" (pp. 35–36). The rule shaped Holmes, Brandeis and Frankfurter (p. 35).
- Valuable and "crucial to any accommodation between judicial review and the democratic faith" (p. 37).
- Bickel splits rational-basis review into three parts: whether the subject falls under a head of power, whether the means fit the purpose, and whether the law accommodates a rights limitation. On the third, Thayer's rule breaks down: "The real question may be whether it is good" (pp. 38–39).
- *Wieman v. Updegraff* (1952): an Oklahoma loyalty oath was struck down as irrational. The relevant line runs "between rationality and will… between rationality and uncontrolled emotion" (p. 41).
- Whether loyalty may be a condition of public employment at all, or the birth-control question in Harlan's *Poe v. Ullman* dissent, needs value judgments beyond rationality (pp. 42–43). Bickel's restatement: "What is rational, and rests on an unquestioned, shared choice of values, is constitutional" (p. 43).
- Thayer's own essays on legal tender and colonies show a 19th-century gentleman's unexamined value consensus (pp. 43–45). His rule is "simply not enough" for modern problems (p. 45).

**Hand's rule of the successful operation of the venture at hand (pp. 46–49)**
- Hand's 1958 Holmes Lectures: review is justified only "to prevent the failure of the venture at hand," that is, policing the frontiers between departments, not the propriety of choices within them (pp. 46–47).
- Applying the vague Bill of Rights makes the Court a "third legislative chamber," and Hand could see no basis for it "except as a *coup de main*" (p. 48).
- Bickel calls this "the therapy of nearly total abstinence." It ignores both the historical evidence and the presumption in favor of existing practice: "his diagnosis was lameness, and his cure amputation" (pp. 48–49).

**Wechsler's rule of the neutral principles (pp. 49–65)**
- Wechsler's 1959 lecture: adjudication must be "genuinely principled," resting on reasons "quite transcending the immediate result," and on "its generality and its neutrality" (pp. 49–50).
- Bickel accepts this as indispensable. *Shelton v. Tucker* (1960), on an Arkansas law requiring teachers to list all their organizations and decided 5–4, failed it: "Such a decision lacks neutral principle because it lacks intellectual coherence" (p. 53). It merely revised "a mere judgment of expediency" (p. 54). Frankfurter's "kadi under a tree" (p. 55).
- But neutrality does not say *which* values the Court may enforce (p. 55).
- Wechsler doubted that *Brown* rested on a neutral principle, framing it as a choice between freedom to associate and freedom not to associate (p. 56). Bickel replies that a principle forbidding the state to impose "permanent, humiliating inferiority" is neutral, and that it rests on objective notice of social fact, not on findings of motive (pp. 56–57).
- Wechsler's deeper demand is for principles applied "across the board and without compromise." That is "absolute application of absolute—even if sometimes flexible—principles," and in a free society "there can be but very few such principles" (p. 59).
- The "benevolent quota" hypothetical, a public housing project reserving 33.3% of units for Black tenants, shows the bind (pp. 60–63). Strict neutrality would force the Court either to impose a "rigidly doctrinaire rule" or to uphold segregation (pp. 63–64).
- "No good society can be unprincipled; and no viable society can be principle-ridden" (p. 64). "The role of principle, when it cannot be the immutable governing rule, is to affect the tendency of policies of expediency" (p. 64).
- Along the way: legislative "motive" is inscrutable. In constitutional law, "purpose" is usually either the Court's own assessment of a measure's effects or a conclusion drawn from its own principled judgment (pp. 61–63).

**The Lincolnian tension (pp. 65–72)**
- Drawing on Jaffa's *Crisis of the House Divided*, Lincoln held the principle that no man may govern another without consent as absolute. He tolerated slavery where it existed but would not accept *Dred Scott* or Douglas's "don't care" policy, which removed the goal of "ultimate extinction" (pp. 65–68).
- "Our democratic system of government exists in this Lincolnian tension between principle and expediency, and within it judicial review must play its role" (p. 68).
- The threefold power (pp. 69–70):
  - **Striking down** must be rigorously principled, but the judgment's compelling force reaches only the case. "…while the Court should not surmount the limitation, it must rise above the case" (p. 70).
  - **Legitimating** also inserts the Court into the decisional process (p. 70).
  - **Staying its hand** needs little justification in democratic terms. Meanwhile "the issue of principle remains in abeyance and ripens" through a colloquy (pp. 70–71). Brandeis: "The most important thing we do… is not doing" (p. 71).
- Examples: the Court rightly left antimiscegenation laws open (*Naim v. Naim*, 1956) and should leave benevolent quotas open. "This is not to say that the Supreme Court should legitimate them—only that it should leave their constitutionality undecided" (pp. 71–72).

### Chapter 3 — "The Infirm Glory of the Positive Hour" (pp. 73–110)

The chapter attacks every approach that hides judicial judgment behind the text, behind history, or behind cynicism.

**Marbury's magnetic pull (pp. 73–75).** Marbury "states the problem rather than solves it; moreover, it states the wrong problem" (p. 74). Yet lawyers make cyclical "atavistic regressions" to the idea of a self-applying Constitution (p. 74).

**Conservatives and neo-realists (pp. 75–84)**
- Senator Walter George defended the Court in 1937 as the voice of "the people," then signed the 1956 Southern Manifesto denouncing "naked judicial power" (p. 76).
- Arthur Krock and James Byrnes treat the Constitution like a deed that a good lawyer reads correctly. On that view, "radical" judges amend it (pp. 76–78).
- Neo-realists such as Alpheus Mason share the same adjectival picture of conservative and radical judges, just with approval (pp. 78–79).
- Felix Cohen, the good realist, insisted that judging is a moral question (p. 80). Bickel adds his own point: if judging were only personal preference, judges' "authority over us is totally intolerable" (p. 80).
- Nihilists: John P. Frank holds that "more important than reason is the quality of creativeness." Bickel sums up Frank's advice to the Court, in his own words and without quotation marks, as: Don't just sit there; do something. Anything (p. 82). Thurman Arnold calls legal reasoning the king's clothes (p. 83). Bickel replies that judges do change their minds through reflection (p. 83). Cynicism is, in Henry L. Stimson's phrase, "the only deadly sin," because it is self-validating (p. 84).

**Justice Black's absolutes (pp. 84–98)**
- In his 1960 NYU lecture, Black said the Bill of Rights contains "absolutes," put there "on purpose by men who knew what words meant." He said the balancing view "comes close to the English doctrine of legislative omnipotence" (p. 86).
- Bickel tests the claim against the texts. What is an "admittedly" cruel punishment: the Rosenbergs, Chessman? Even the Fourth Amendment's absolute applies only to the "unreasonable" (pp. 87–89).
- Black returns to Justice Roberts's 1936 "lay the Article… beside the statute" method (p. 90), offering his results "wrapped in [the text's] cellophane, with locked-in flavor" (p. 92).
- **Benevolent illusions.** Borrowing a line from Conrad's *Under Western Eyes*, Bickel says a soothing fiction that the people rule and the text decides "would be no sin," but it is dangerous (p. 92). It is two-edged, as the Southern Manifesto shows, and it may engulf the judges and breed "free-ranging 'activist' government by the judiciary," leading to crises like 1937 (pp. 92–93).
- Charles Black reframes the absolutes as an "attitude" (p. 93). Bickel distinguishes the Court openly announcing principles that shape the tendency of politics, which is legitimate, from an attitude that only works if judges believe an illusion (pp. 95–96). The alarm-clock image: Justice Black "labors to set our alarm clocks one hour ahead," so that we wake at the right time even when we oversleep (p. 96).
- "The process is justified only if it is as deliberate and conscious as men can make it" (p. 96). Bickel adds that "the Constitution is merely words—deathless words, but words. And the future will not be ruled; it can only possibly be persuaded" (p. 98).

**History (pp. 98–110)**
- History's role is "reviving the wonder of the past and relieving its tyranny" (Muller, p. 99).
- Case studies of claimed original intent being refuted (pp. 99–102): Brandeis on the First Amendment versus Levy's *Legacy of Suppression*; the Fourteenth Amendment's framers, who did not intend to outlaw segregation; Black's incorporation thesis in *Adamson* versus Fairman; legal tender (Field versus Thayer); courts-martial (Henderson versus Wiener).
- Asking history for specific answers is "to ask the wrong questions," and the answer is "no answer." The refutations disprove the claims but do not prove the opposite (pp. 102–103).
- The Framers showed, in a phrase of Morison and Commager, a "preference of 'may' over 'must'" (p. 104). In Charles P. Curtis's words, they left "chapter headings," and "They died in a younger world" (p. 104).
- **On constitutional design (pp. 105–106).**
  - A constitution must let future battles "be fought out by men who, on both sides of contested issues, can in good faith profess allegiance to the organic law."
  - In Marshall's words from *McCulloch*, it must not "partake of the prolixity of a legal code."
  - A constitution that tries to settle too much "invites either abandonment or frequent amendment," and "The familiarity of amendment will breed a species of contempt and incapacitate the document for symbolic service" (p. 105).
  - His cautionary example is the Spanish Republic's 1931 constitution, which "contained in the greatest possible detail provisions for the disestablishment of the Spanish Church." Quoting the historian Hugh Thomas, he notes that "all Spanish Catholics were forced into the position of having to oppose the very Constitution of the Republic" (p. 106).
- Sutherland versus Hughes in *Blaisdell* (1934) (p. 106). Brandeis's "living organism" passage was cut from an opinion at Taft's request (pp. 107–108).
- "Like the text, … history cannot displace judgment. And like the text, it should not, if it could" (p. 108). The Court maintains "continuity in the midst of change" and builds what Herbert J. Muller calls a "usable past" (p. 109). We must know our history accurately "in order to be aware that it is our own reasoned and revocable will, not some idealized ancestral compulsion, that moves us forward" (p. 110).

### Chapter 4 — The Passive Virtues (pp. 111–198)

The core of the book. Much discord on the Court comes from deciding more than necessary. The techniques of "not doing" have fallen "into something of a state of disrepair" (p. 112).

**The metaphors of jurisdiction and their sense (pp. 113–127)**
- Origins: *Hayburn's Case* (1792), refusing non-final pension determinations, and the 1793 Correspondence of the Justices, declining Jefferson's request for advisory opinions (pp. 113–114).
- Case or controversy, standing and the ban on advisory opinions follow from Marbury's logic and from Bickel's own justification (pp. 114–115). Concrete cases let the Court judge after laws have met what Frankfurter called the "impact of actuality." The time lag "cushions the clash" with the legislature (pp. 115–116). He notes that English courts and some U.S. state courts *do* give advisory opinions (p. 115).
- Frankfurter: "The Court is not the forum for a chivalrous or disinterested defense of the Constitution" (p. 120). Cases include *Ashwander*, *Tennessee Electric Power v. TVA* and *Frothingham* (the taxpayer suit) (pp. 119–122).
- Marshall in *Cohens v. Virginia*: declining jurisdiction that has been given "would be treason to the constitution" (p. 117). The Court routinely does exactly that through "impure" standing, ripeness, the political question doctrine, certiorari and dismissals of appeals (pp. 118–127).
- Wechsler treats the political question doctrine as constitutional interpretation that the matter is committed to another branch. Bickel disagrees: it is "something of prudence, not construction and not principle," which "cannot exist within the four corners of *Marbury* v. *Madison*" (pp. 125–126).

**The power to decline jurisdiction (pp. 127–133)**
- There are two ways to keep review within democratic bounds: limit the occasions for intervention, or restrict the kinds of principle the Court may enforce. Wechsler takes the second (p. 128).
- That path ignores legitimation. A declaration that a law is "not unconstitutional" is "a significant intervention in the political process, different in degree only" from striking it down (p. 129). Examples: federal aid to parochial schools, and pre-1954 segregation in the North (p. 130). Jackson's *Korematsu* dissent: once the Court validates it, "The principle then lies about like a loaded weapon" (p. 131).
- "No attempt to lift the Court out of the Lincolnian tension can be successful" (p. 131).
- The techniques "cannot themselves be principled"; this "is where the Court is most a political animal" (p. 132). Freund on the *Steel Seizure* case (p. 132). But this is "not to concede unchanneled… discretion": the standard is prudence (pp. 132–133).

**Restraint, prior and judicial: Times Film v. Chicago (1961) (pp. 133–143)**
- A distributor refused to submit the film "Don Juan" to Chicago's censor and sued to challenge all prior restraint. The Court upheld the censorship, 5–4.
- Standing was clear (p. 135), but the case was framed abstractly to force an all-or-nothing answer. Borrowing a figure from Paul Freund, Bickel concludes that "the real issues must be dealt with at retail, whereas the parties here offered one issue at wholesale" (p. 139).
- The historical case against prior restraint (Crown censors versus juries) "has only the faintest present relevance." Today the problem is "protection of minorities against a majority in a working, deep-cutting democracy" (p. 136).
- Prudence may justify *withholding* legitimation, but never legitimating or striking down. Japanese relocation is his example of a measure the Court should not have legitimated (p. 139). By ignoring a ruling's likely effects, the Court "merely abandons control of the direction" its decisions push opinion (p. 140).
- Proclaiming a valid goal that is unattainable for now is legitimate. Proclaiming an untenable absolute involves "manipulative dissimulation" (p. 141).
- Right disposition: deny certiorari, or dismiss for lack of ripeness after argument. He also questions the rule of four (pp. 142–143).

**Ripeness and the birth-control colloquy: Poe v. Ullman (1961) (pp. 143–156)**
- The case was fully ripe, but the *issue* was not (p. 143). Connecticut's 1879 ban had never been enforced against users, and drugstore sales had never been prosecuted. The only enforcement was two vending-machine prosecutions and a 1940 prosecution of two doctors and a nurse who ran a birth-control clinic. Repeal efforts since 1923 had failed (pp. 145–147).
- Frankfurter at argument: "So the matter is entirely academic." Counsel: "I suppose so" (p. 146). The statute was "law by default," a deadlock of wills (p. 147).
- Abstention could "help to set in motion the process of political decision." A vote against repeal is not the same as a vote to enact (pp. 147–148).
- **Desuetude** is the device implicit in Frankfurter's opinion: a long-unenforced statute cannot suddenly be revived. Bickel cites Julianus via Gray (pp. 148–149). It is kin to the void-for-vagueness doctrine, following Amsterdam's analysis. A vague statute delegates to unaccountable officials, and the Court in effect "asks that the *legislature* do it, if it is to be done at all" (pp. 149–152). "Responsible government, however, is a function of imbalance" (p. 154).
- If Catholic opinion in Connecticut and responsive officials cannot decide the question, "it is quite wrong for the Court to relieve them of this burden of self-government." Judicial review should not dwarf political capacity "knowingly, demonstrably, avoidably" (p. 156).

**Political responsibility and congressional investigations (pp. 156–164)**
- *Rumely* (1953) read a House resolution narrowly "in the candid service of avoiding a serious constitutional doubt." Limits should be drawn only after Congress "has demonstrated its full awareness of what is at stake by unequivocally authorizing an inquiry of dubious limits" (p. 157).
- *Watkins* (1957): "Who can define the meaning of 'un-American'?" The real ground, in the Court's own words, was that the House itself had never made the "critical judgment," leaving "a wide gulf between the responsibility for the use of investigative power and the actual exercise of that power" (pp. 157–159). *Barenblatt* (1959, 5–4), *Wilkinson* and *Braden* (1961) retreated and legitimated the House Un-American Activities Committee (p. 159).
- **Delegation doctrine restated** (pp. 159–161). Discredited by *Panama Refining* and *Schechter* (1935), it is really about "the crucial joinder between power and broadly based democratic responsibility" (p. 160), and it applies to congressional committees too. "The more fundamental the issue, the nearer it is to principle, the more important it is that it be decided in the first instance by the legislature" (p. 161). "Its end is to pose a question, not to impose an answer" (p. 161).
- The investigative power necessarily suspends many ordinary rules. As the Court said in *Watkins*, "Only the legislative assembly initiating an investigation can assay the relative necessity of specific disclosures." Bickel adds: "The Court can see that it does" (p. 163). *Hannah v. Larche* (the Civil Rights Commission's procedures) should have been dismissed as unripe (pp. 163–164).

**Security dismissals and passports (pp. 164–169)**
- *Kent v. Dulles* (1958): denying passports to Communists was held not authorized by statute. This was "remanding to Congress for a second look," asking for explicit reconsideration of a decision made "back-handedly, off-handedly" (pp. 164–166).
- *Greene v. McElroy* (1959): a clearance revoked on secret evidence without confrontation was held unauthorized (p. 166).
- *Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy* (1961): a short-order cook was barred from a naval gun factory without charges or hearing, and the Court upheld it. Bickel: "A short-order cook, after all, should have as much right to her humble job as Mr. Greene has to his career." Constructing such exceptions is not the Court's function (pp. 166–169).

**The well-tempered case and the choice of device (pp. 169–183)**
- The devices differ in intrinsic significance. Certiorari is neutral; vagueness and delegation rank high, and desuetude "at something of an apex" (pp. 169–170).
- Naturally developed lawsuits usually offer a choice of grounds. Even *Marbury* could have been decided by statutory construction, and the school cases could have been decided on unequal facilities. In *Brown* the Court chose to announce the principle it had ripened over earlier cases (pp. 171–172). Cases framed to force the Court's hand deserve little sympathy when dismissed (p. 172).
- **The Supreme Court is not an ordinary court.** Courts of general jurisdiction "must, indeed, resolve all controversies within their jurisdiction, because the alternative is chaos." The Supreme Court in constitutional cases renders "an additional, principled judgment on what has already been authoritatively ordered." "Fixation on an individual right to judgment by the Supreme Court is, therefore, largely question-begging" (p. 173). Answering Justice Black's claim that Congress "has a right" to a decision: Congress "has no rights; it has functions" (p. 174).
- Defended dismissals: *Naim* (antimiscegenation, argued as unwise "just then") and *Shelton*, which should have been declined (p. 174).
- *Garner v. Louisiana* (1961), the first sit-in case. First cases are generally poor vehicles, and letting a problem "simmer" works like a trial balloon (p. 176). The statute involved was also outdated (p. 176). The convictions were reversed on the narrow, somewhat "idiosyncratic" ground of no evidence of public alarm, which amounted to a "colloquy with the trial court" (pp. 177–179).
- Vagueness can be applied to a single application of a statute and can operate as a canon of construction, as with the Sherman Act and labor, the civil-rights statutes of 1866 and 1870, *Rumely* and *Kent*. Bickel adds that it "should have been" used to construe the Mann Act, and was not (pp. 180–182). State courts act as intermediaries in colloquies with state legislatures (p. 182). "There are no set rules of selection" (p. 183).

**The political question and the resources of rhetoric (pp. 183–198)**
- The political question doctrine is the culmination of the devices (p. 183). Its foundation is "the Court's sense of lack of capacity," compounded of four elements (p. 184):
  - "(a) the strangeness of the issue and its intractability to principled resolution;"
  - "(b) the sheer momentousness of it, which tends to unbalance judicial judgment;"
  - "(c) the anxiety, not so much that the judicial judgment will be ignored, as that perhaps it should but will not be;"
  - "(d) finally ('in a mature democracy'), the inner vulnerability, the self-doubt of an institution which is electorally irresponsible and has no earth to draw strength from."
- Examples: *Dred Scott* (of which Maurice Finkelstein said, "A question which involved a Civil War can hardly be proper material for the wrangling of lawyers"), *Luther v. Borden*, and Justice Bradley's deciding vote on the 1877 Electoral Commission (pp. 184–185).
- **Two kinds of political question** (pp. 185–187), both described in phrases Bickel takes from Jaffe:
  - Matters "better done without rules," which are unprincipled on principle: recognizing foreign governments, abrogating treaties, defining the general welfare, excluding aliens, the President firing a cabinet officer. The Court can affirm this discretion by principled adjudication (p. 186).
  - Matters where principles exist but are "only among the numerous relevant considerations," such as security dismissals and investigations. There the Court may yield to necessity, "in contravention of principle, if necessary," as with delayed school integration (pp. 185–187).
- Even when yielding, the Court can teach. "And the Court's arguments need not be compulsory in order to be compelling." It can "speak over their heads to the nation" (p. 188).
- The limit: "If the political institutions at last insist upon a course of action that cannot be accommodated to principle, it is no part of the function of the Court to bless it, however double-negatively" (p. 188). The remedy is outright dismissal, or in contempt cases, requiring Congress to use its own process at the bar of each House (p. 189).
- **Apportionment.** *Colegrove v. Green* (1946): "Courts ought not to enter this political thicket" (p. 190). Bickel argues it is not a standing case, not a case of textual commitment to Congress, and not about difficulty of enforcement (pp. 190–191). Equal representation is an aspiration, but it cannot be proclaimed with the moral certainty of the anti-segregation principle. Representation must also reflect interests, groups and regions, and Bickel asks "Who… favors proportional representation?" with Weimar and the Fourth Republic in mind (pp. 192–193). Apportionment is "a very high percentage of politics with a very small admixture of definable principle. In race relations, the proportions are reversed" (p. 193). That is why the political question doctrine was not even mentioned in *Brown* (pp. 193–194).
- *Baker v. Carr* (March 1962): Tennessee had not reapportioned in 60 years. The Court "opened a colloquy" rather than taking over apportionment, which would have been "too much like playing de Gaulle to the American Republic" (pp. 195–196). The danger ahead is legitimating some apportionment scheme, which Bickel calls "Plessy v. Ferguson's Error" (p. 197).
- Choices about whether and when to decide are not principled adjudication, but they still require disinterestedness, which is reinforced by public argument, written reasons and professional scrutiny (pp. 197–198).

### Chapter 5 — Neither Force nor Will (pp. 199–243)

**Recap and refinement (pp. 199–207)**
- Tocqueville: "Hardly any question arises in the United States that is not resolved sooner or later into a judicial question" (p. 199).
- "But it would be intolerable for the Court finally to govern all that it touches, for that would turn us into a Platonic kingdom contrary to the morality of self-government; and in this world at least, it would not work" (p. 200).
- Assuming the Court must always decide leads to manipulation, to second-guessing on expediency, or to both (p. 200).
- **Constitutional adjudication defined** (pp. 202–203). It is a decision that "checks the will of the political institutions, now and for an indeterminate future, or… legitimates their action," on the basis of principle, and that only the Court itself can revise. Decisions that bind must be principled. The passive devices instead leave the legislature free to remake its decision, and what they check is "often" a decision "actually made by officials." Officials "stand in closer relation to the electoral process than does the Court, but there is not between them and the Court the gulf that exists between the latter and the truly representative institutions" (p. 202).
- The judges have, as Hamilton said, "neither force nor will," only "society's striving for the rule of principle, its readiness to receive principle from the Court, and its strong, habit-formed inclination to accept… to obey" (p. 204).
- **Three ways to erode the rule of judicial principle** (p. 204):
  - "the attempt to push its dominion past natural limits"
  - "manipulation… of principles that are not tenable for their own sakes"
  - "to call the name of principle in vain, until even its genuine invocation ceases to produce the reflex of acceptance"
- Every decision must be "self-conscious… intelligible, rational, candid" and disinterested. When dismissing, the judges are "excused from articulating reasons, but not from reason itself" (p. 205).
- The passive devices are "lesser rational alternatives to an otherwise unavoidable principled judgment." The authority to use them derives from the Court's ultimate function (pp. 205–206).
- Legislative inertia gives the devices an "active edge," but Congress often answers and reverses the Court, engaging in "responsive readings," a phrase Bickel takes from a 1962 *Yale Law Journal* comment (p. 206 and its note 5). All of these are points on a "continuum of judicial power," from a denial of certiorari to *Brown* (p. 207).

**The mysteries of motive (pp. 208–221)**
- Justices Black and Brennan proposed invalidating investigations whose "dominant purpose" was exposure for its own sake (p. 208). The traditional rule (Marshall, Cooley, Cardozo) forbids inquiry into legislative motive (pp. 208–209).
- Motive must be distinguished from "purpose," which is either "the name given to the Court's objective assessment of the effect of a statute" or "a conclusionary term denoting the Court's independent judgment of the constitutionally allowable end that the legislature could have had in view" (p. 209).
- *Gomillion v. Lightfoot* (1960) concerned the Tuskegee boundary redrawn into a "strangely irregular 28-sided figure" that removed nearly every Black voter (p. 210). In Justice Frankfurter's words for the Court, the evidence was "tantamount . . . to a mathematical demonstration" that the line was drawn to fence Black citizens out of town. So the forbidden purpose could be found objectively (p. 211). Compare *Guinn* (the grandfather clause) and *Lane v. Wilson*: the Fifteenth Amendment "nullifies sophisticated as well as simple-minded modes of discrimination" (p. 212). *Yick Wo* (1886) involved administration "with an evil eye and an unequal hand" (pp. 212–213).
- "Legislative motives are nearly always mixed and nearly never professed" (p. 214). Cross-examining legislators would be "unthinkable" and would breed routine perjury (p. 215). Motive review allows no colloquy, because the legislature cannot cure a bad motive. "It binds finally, without saying so and without being able to say why" (pp. 216–217).
- **Officials are different.** Their motives are provable, as in the New Rochelle school case (*Taylor v. Board of Education*, 1961), which matters for pupil-placement laws (pp. 217–218). The Court gives "the responsible political institutions a degree of deference that it does not owe to officials" (p. 214).
- On the *Child Labor Tax Case*'s complaint that all could see the bad motive: "the Court ought to shut its mind to much of what all others think they see. That is precisely what courts are for" (p. 220).

**The mirage of equal protection (pp. 221–228)**
- *Railway Express v. New York* (1949): Jackson's concurrence argued that requiring laws to apply generally is the best guarantee against arbitrary government (pp. 222–223).
- Bickel: as a general doctrine this has only a "spurious attraction." Legislatures may make "arational" choices favoring economic groups such as farmers, truckers and Times Square advertisers. Race and religion are exempt from that marketplace (pp. 223–227).
- Where principles of the first magnitude are at stake, though, the Jackson approach can serve as a device. Bickel's own hypotheticals: a state closing its public schools only in cities of 100,000 or more, or a federal law allowing warrantless home searches for atomic materials except in communities under 100,000. Requiring broader application then makes government "turn square corners." But the "candid service" is performed only "when the Court is candid in the performance" (pp. 227–228).

**The square corners of the Commerce Clause and procedural due process (pp. 228–234)**
- Freund: "governments may do things in a right way and not in a wrong" way (p. 228). Commerce Clause cases enforce a "common market" (pp. 228–229). Powell: the states may tax commerce, "but not too much" (p. 229).
- These decisions bind the states but not Congress, which can always respond. That possibility of congressional reprise is what makes the Court's pragmatic, sometimes unprincipled performance in this area tolerable. Court stewardship of apportionment would have no such recourse (pp. 230–231).
- Most such decisions regulate methods, not ends, and so do many free-speech cases on sound trucks and canvassing (pp. 231–233). Procedural due process concerns the "how" rather than the "what" (p. 233). Jackson preferred "Soviet substantive law applied in good faith by our common-law procedures" to the reverse (pp. 233–234). But some procedural rules, such as a probable-cause requirement for investigations, would in effect foreclose ends (p. 234).

**"The Judgment of This Court" (pp. 235–243)**
- As Brandeis wrote in 1936, the Court has "avoided passing upon a large part of all the constitutional questions thrust upon it for decision" (p. 235). But a "residue of ultimates" remains. "How and whence do nine lawyers, holding lifetime appointments, devise or derive principles which they are prepared to impose without recourse upon a democratic society?" (p. 235).
- Text, history and precedent "are the setting for judgment and they condition it, but they are not its wellspring" (p. 236). Frankfurter's *Sweezy* concurrence: judgment "must rest on fundamental presuppositions rooted in history to which widespread acceptance may fairly be attributed… the judgment of this Court" (p. 236).
- Presuppositions must be "traced and evaluated from the roots up," with Brandeis's microscope and telescope (p. 237). Bickel infers "that the life of the law is reason, and *that* in no narrow or technical sense either." Following Dwight Macdonald on literary judgment, a sound principle "cannot be proved" but "can be demonstrated" (p. 237). Brandeis's *Olmstead* dissent and *Brown* are demonstrable; maximum-hours and minimum-wage invalidations were not (pp. 237–238).
- Frankfurter's "widespread acceptance" does not mean declaring an existing consensus, which political institutions do better. It means principles that will gain assent "in a rather immediate foreseeable future." Hence "the obligation to succeed" (p. 239).
- Timing can be controlled. Through "a continuing colloquy with the political institutions and with society at large," the Court shapes and reduces the question before deciding it (p. 240).
- **Contrast between segregation and the death penalty** (pp. 240–243). For a generation the Court eroded segregation before *Brown*. On capital punishment it missed its chances: Louisiana's second electrocution (*Francis v. Resweber*), the insanity cases, the Rosenbergs, Chessman. Bickel says it should have held that executing Chessman in 1960, after years on death row, was unconstitutional, to start a process of change (p. 243).

### Chapter 6 — The Supreme Court at the Bar of Politics (pp. 244–272)

*Brown* "at once epitomize[s] and challenge[s]" the thesis. He has argued "that principle may be a universal guide but not a universal constraint" and "that principle is evolved conversationally not perfected unilaterally" (p. 244). A second colloquy, and a second kind of reprise, can come *after* judgment. Applied principle often lasts only "a generation or two" (p. 244).

**Composing for the anthologies (pp. 245–247)**
- On May 17, 1954 the Court announced the principle without a decree. The announcement alone placed segregation, in Lincoln's phrase about slavery, "in course of ultimate extinction." By early 1955 more than 500 districts had desegregated, affecting about 250,000 Black pupils (pp. 245–246).
- But the Court does not sit "to compose for the anthologies" (Freund). It is a court of law and must issue decrees (pp. 246–247).

**All deliberate speed (pp. 247–254)**
- "The matrix paradox": the Court may decide only concrete cases, yet it may decide constitutional issues only on general principle (p. 247). The five cases therefore implicated about 5,000 districts (p. 248).
- Reasons for gradual relief:
  - Administrative complexity, and the Court's own responsibility, since segregation had flourished in reliance on *Plessy* (pp. 248–249). John W. Davis argued that a principle can reach "a moment of repose"; Bickel rejects that as static but takes the reliance seriously (p. 249).
  - Equity's tradition of discretionary accommodation alongside common-law principle (pp. 249–250).
  - Most of all, the opposition of a regional majority. Even if the Court were, in Mr. Dooley's phrase, to follow the "election returns," the relevant returns are national, not regional (p. 250). Principles must "gain assent, not necessarily to have it" (p. 251). Unlike the criminal law, which needs enforcing only against a small antisocial minority, the task was "not to punish law breakers but to diminish their number" (p. 251).
  - Need for the elected branches. "In an enforcement crisis of any real proportions, the judiciary is wholly dependent upon the Executive" (p. 252). Political institutions are "uncomfortable in the presence of hard and fast principles calling for universal and sudden execution," so leeway was an invitation to join "a major enterprise of social reform" (p. 252).
- The NAACP urged immediate "shock treatment." The Solicitor General urged plans, with no deadlines, because "maximum periods tend to become minimum periods" (pp. 252–253).
- Bickel traces the phrase "all deliberate speed" to Francis Thompson's "The Hound of Heaven," to equity's "all *convenient* speed," and to Byron and Goethe (p. 253). It meant the Court would return to "passive receptiveness" and "a continual colloquy," answering no only when a proposed expedient amounted to abandoning the principle (p. 254).

**Political warfare and the uses of decisions of courts (pp. 254–272)**
- Initial Southern reactions were mostly restrained ("Arkansas will obey the law. It always has," p. 255). By 1958 the issue looked like "the American Algeria." Little Rock followed, schools closed, and Congress and the presidency were immobilized (pp. 255–256).
- The turning point was the Southern Manifesto of March 11, 1956, "a calculated declaration of political war." In form it relied on Marbury-style literalism; in substance it claimed segregation was right (pp. 256–258).
- **The decisive insight** (p. 258). As the southern leaders realized, the Court's law "could not in our system prevail… if it ran counter to deeply felt popular needs or convictions, or even if it was opposed by a determined and substantial minority and received with indifference by the rest of the country. This, in the end, is how and why judicial review is consistent with the theory and practice of political democracy. This is why the Supreme Court is a court of last resort presumptively only." Powell: the Court can only "say something. The effect depends upon others." "Broad and sustained application of the Court's law, when challenged, is a function of its rightness, not merely of its pronouncement."
- **Lincoln on Dred Scott** (pp. 259–261). Both quotations are on p. 260: "We offer no *resistance* to it" as a decree, but "we nevertheless do oppose that decision as a political rule" binding on voters, Congress and the President. Lincoln would vote to bar slavery in a territory despite the decision (p. 260). "What are the uses of decisions of courts?" (pp. 260–261). This was "heresy against the theoretical basis of *Marbury* v. *Madison*" (p. 261).
- **Two qualifications on deference to the Court** (p. 261). First, "on the supreme occasion, when the system is forced to find ultimate self-consistency, the principle of self-rule must prevail." Second, separation of powers is not machinery: the branches' effectiveness depends on "the sweaty intimacy of creatures locked in combat."
- Precedents: Jackson on the Bank; Taney as Attorney General in 1832, saying the Court's reasoning is not binding on the other branches; FDR and the Guffey Coal bill (pp. 262–263).
- Southern politicians were "perfectly within their rights" to reject *Brown* as a political rule, argue against it and relitigate it, while obeying specific decrees (pp. 263–264).
- Eisenhower and the Court's *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958) answered with Marbury and judicial supremacy (pp. 264–265). "Both sides were right, both sides were wrong, and it didn't matter." The real question was whether the decision was right, and the President declined to defend it on the merits (p. 265).
- Mob violence was the South's crucial mistake. "For enforcement is a crisis of the system, not its norm. When the law summons force to its aid, it demonstrates, not its strength and stability, but its weakness and impermanence" (p. 266). Television images of mobs facing Black children "concretized the abstraction of racism" and roused Northern opinion (pp. 266–267).
- "This was one time when hiding behind the judges' skirts would not do. The political institutions had a decision of their own to make" (p. 268). The 1960 campaign and the Kennedy administration assumed political responsibility, which let Southern moderates act once the issue was settled (p. 268).
- Ole Miss (September 1962) was mishandled by delay but confirmed the settlement. Insurrection must be met "decisively and promptly, so that the futility of resistance is never in the slightest doubt" (pp. 268–271).
- What remains: the economic problems of the Black Belt, and the nationalizing of the issue through Northern de facto segregation. "The goals we acknowledge determine the direction in which our society moves" (pp. 271–272).

## Bickel's map of limits on the judicial branch (comparison checklist)

### External limits (structural and political)

- **No force, no purse.** Hamilton's "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment" (epigraph). The Court depends on the executive for enforcement (p. 252).
- **Political non-acceptance.** The elected branches and the public may reject a ruling's principle while obeying its decree (pp. 258–264). Sustained opposition can nullify doctrine "directly or by desuetude" (p. 28).
- **Constitutional amendment.** An "extreme minority veto," so it is rarely a practical check (p. 21).
- **Congressional control of appellate jurisdiction.** An embarrassment for Marbury-style theory (pp. 124–125, 127).
- **Appointments over time.** Justices are "time bombs, not warheads" (p. 31). Bickel gives exceptions, where appointments changed doctrine quickly (pp. 31–32):
  - Grant's two appointments in 1870, which reversed a recent decision on federal monetary powers. Note 33 cites Fairman on the Legal Tender Cases.
  - Justice Black's arrival in 1937.
- **Congressional reprise.** Possible after statutory construction and Commerce Clause decisions, which is what makes them tolerable (pp. 206, 230–231).
- **The finality question.** Wellington's foreword stresses that a decision is not final "as a political rule" until accepted (p. xii).

### Self-imposed doctrinal limits

- **Thayer's clear-mistake rule** (pp. 35–46). Deference on rationality.
- **Neutral principles** (pp. 49–65). Required for decisions on the merits, but not as Wechsler's "across the board" absolutes.
- **Case or controversy, standing, no advisory opinions, finality** (pp. 113–122).
- **Ripeness** of the case and of the issue (pp. 123–124, 133–156).
- **Discretionary certiorari and dismissal of appeals** (pp. 126–127).
- **Political question doctrine,** in its two kinds (pp. 183–197).
- **Vagueness, delegation, and "remanding to Congress for a second look"** (pp. 149–166, 180–182).
- **Desuetude** (pp. 148–156).
- **Narrow construction and procedural holdings** (pp. 175–183).
- **Avoiding motive inquiry into legislatures** (pp. 208–221).
- **Remedial timing** ("all deliberate speed") (pp. 247–254).

### Limits of craft and character

- Reasoned, public, written opinions, argued with by the profession: "judge and company" (Bentham, p. 240).
- Disinterestedness, candor, and rejection of "benevolent illusions" (pp. 92–98, 197–198, 205).
- A nine-member Court, because fundamental presuppositions differ (p. 238).
- The "obligation to succeed": principles must be able to win assent (p. 239).

## Cast of characters

- **James Bradley Thayer** (1893). Strike only clear mistakes. Warns that review dwarfs the people's political capacity (pp. 21–22, 35–46).
- **Learned Hand** (1958 Holmes Lectures). Review limited to keeping departments within their frontiers. Rights review is a "*coup de main*" (pp. 46–49).
- **Herbert Wechsler** (1959, "Toward Neutral Principles of Constitutional Law"). Principled adjudication and doubts about *Brown*. No discretion to decline jurisdiction (pp. 49–65, 118–119, 125, 128–129).
- **Charles L. Black Jr.** Review legitimates as well as checks. Recasts Justice Black's absolutes as an "attitude" (pp. 29–31, 93–98).
- **Justice Hugo Black.** Literal absolutes in the Bill of Rights; incorporation in *Adamson* (pp. 84–102).
- **Justice Felix Frankfurter.** Restraint; *Poe v. Ullman*; *Colegrove*; the *Sweezy* concurrence's "judgment of this Court" (pp. 146–154, 190, 236).
- **Justice Louis Brandeis.** His *Ashwander* concurrence on standing; his 1936 remark on avoiding constitutional questions; "The most important thing we do… is not doing"; the *Olmstead* dissent (pp. 71, 119–120, 235, 237).
- **Realists, neo-realists and nihilists:** Felix Cohen, Alpheus Mason, John P. Frank, Thurman Arnold (pp. 75–84).
- **Abraham Lincoln,** through Jaffa. Principle combined with compromise; *Dred Scott* not accepted as a political rule (pp. 65–68, 259–261).
- **Paul Freund.** "turn square corners"; "compose for the anthologies"; his critique of the *Steel Seizure* case (pp. 132, 228, 246).

## Reception and later developments (outside the book)

- **Wellington's 1986 foreword** (in the book) names the *finality* of constitutional decisions as the neglected core of Bickel's view (p. xii).
- **Gerald Gunther,** "The Subtle Vices of the 'Passive Virtues'—A Comment on Principle and Expediency in Judicial Review," 64 *Columbia Law Review* 1 (1964), is the classic critique. It charges that Bickel's discretionary avoidance lets expediency in through jurisdictional doctrine.
- **John Hart Ely,** *Democracy and Distrust* (1980), and **Jesse Choper,** *Judicial Review and the National Political Process* (1980), take up the counter-majoritarian problem, as Wellington notes.
- **Predictions later overtaken:**
  - *Poe*'s statute was struck in *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965), the clinic prosecution Bickel saw coming (p. 147).
  - Antimiscegenation laws, which he thought should be left open in 1956, were struck in *Loving v. Virginia* (1967).
  - His doubt that equal representation could be a judicial principle (pp. 192–193) was overtaken by *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964), "one person, one vote."
  - On film censorship, *Freedman v. Maryland* (1965) imposed procedural safeguards on the censor, close to Bickel's suggestions at pp. 137–138.
  - On capital punishment, which he thought a generation away (p. 242), *Furman v. Georgia* (1972) came within a decade, followed by *Gregg v. Georgia* (1976).
  - His benevolent-quota discussion (pp. 58–72) anticipates the later affirmative-action cases.

### First test: does Bickel carry over to a parliamentary system?

Bickel wrote about a presidential, federal system with separately elected branches. Israel is parliamentary and unitary. Before using any bridge below, it helps to sort his arguments into those that depend on the American structure and those that do not.

**Arguments that lean on the American structure**

- **A separately elected executive.**
  - Bickel rejects the argument that the difficulty disappears because legislatures are themselves unrepresentative: "nothing in these complexities can alter the essential reality that judicial review is a deviant institution in the American democracy." Unequal representation in the legislature is partly corrected, he says, because "statutes are the product of the legislature and the executive acting in concert, and that the executive represents a very different constituency and thus tends to cure inequities of over- and underrepresentation" (p. 18).
  - Representation is spread across "a troika of institutions, each answering to a differently weighted constituency, with the executive's normally being the most straight-out majoritarian" (p. 193). He notes that "The only partial exceptions are unicameral Nebraska and unit-vote Georgia."
  - In Israel the government serves with the confidence of a unicameral Knesset, so the legislative and executive majorities are normally one coalition.
  - What it teaches: the Court faces a single fused majority, with no separately elected executive to correct it. That may enlarge both Bickel's justification (no institution outside the coalition is placed to hold to principle) and his difficulty (the Court is overriding the only elected organ).
- **An executive that enforces with its own mandate.**
  - "In an enforcement crisis of any real proportions, the judiciary is wholly dependent upon the Executive" (p. 252). Lincoln's "political rule" leaves voters, Congress and the President free to oppose a decision's principle while its decree is obeyed (pp. 259–264).
  - In Israel the executive that must carry out a ruling is often the coalition whose own measure was struck down.
  - What it teaches: Bickel's dependence on the Executive is sharpest exactly where the parliamentary system puts the losing party in charge of enforcement.
- **A constitution that is very hard to amend.**
  - Bickel rejects the argument that the people have consented to judicial review because the Amending Clause "has been employed to reverse the work of the Court only twice, perhaps three times." His answer: "the Amending Clause itself incorporates an extreme minority veto" (p. 21).
  - The same feature makes the Court's constitutional rulings hard to undo by amendment. That is part of why the counter-majoritarian difficulty is serious in America.
  - In Israel most Basic Law provisions can be amended by an ordinary Knesset majority. A few are entrenched, such as the electoral-system section of Basic Law: The Knesset, which needs 61 votes.
  - What it teaches: the worry partly flips. It is less about the Court having the last word and more about how quickly a coalition can rewrite the constitution in reply. That is the setting of the Court's review of Basic Laws in *Shafir* (2021) and in the January 2024 ruling (bridges 5 and 6).
- **Proportional representation.**
  - In the apportionment discussion Bickel calls equality of representation "one goal, and the only principled one, among many," and says "no principled way" of accommodating it to the others "has as yet been discovered" (pp. 192–193).
  - He then asks: "Who, after all, remembering the Weimar Republic or the Fourth French Republic, favors proportional representation?" (p. 193).
  - The Knesset is elected by nationwide proportional representation, so it comes close to the equal individual vote that the American system modifies.
  - What it teaches: on Bickel's p. 18 reasoning, a Knesset elected this way gives the counter-majoritarian difficulty at least as much force as Congress does. His p. 193 remark points the other way. He wants institutions that are not "so fragmented or finely balanced as to be incapable of decisive action" (p. 192), and his examples of proportional representation are Weimar and the Fourth Republic. Either way, the American correctives he relies on are absent.
- **Federalism.**
  - Bickel's strongest textual case is review of *state* acts, so that federal law is uniform (pp. 10–14). Even there, he says, the Court "represents the national will against local particularism; but it does not represent it, as the Congress does, through electoral responsibility" (p. 33).
  - State legislatures are among his colloquy partners, with state courts acting "as intermediaries—quite literally, as interpreters" (p. 182).
  - Israel is unitary.
  - What it teaches: Bickel's best-grounded justification has no Israeli counterpart, so the Israeli case must rest on his functional argument from principle (pp. 23–28).

**Bickel's own parliamentary references**

- **Thayer's worry, tested on Britain.** Thayer feared that judicial review would lead legislatures to leave constitutional questions to the courts. Bickel says that "what Thayer observed remains observable" and cites Herman Finer's comparison of British and American legislative investigations: "Though Parliament is sovereign and can legally do anything it likes, its practices are kinder, more restrained, and less invasive of the rights of those who come under its investigative attention" (p. 22).
- **Legislative omnipotence.** Justice Black charged that treating all constitutional questions as matters of "reasonableness, proximity, and degree" "comes close to the English doctrine of legislative omnipotence." Bickel found that closeness "open to considerable question" (p. 86). Compare the Israeli reasonableness debate (bridge 7).
- **The Crown.** Britain is "the most potent historical demonstration of the efficaciousness of a symbol, made concrete in the person of the Crown." The President serves that function "only very marginally," because "the personification of unity must be above the political battle." Since "The effective Presidents have of necessity been men of power," Bickel says, "it has in large part been left to the Supreme Court to concretize the symbol of the Constitution" (p. 31). He offers this "as an observation, not as justification" (p. 31). Israel, like Britain, has a ceremonial head of state outside the political battle.
- **Advisory opinions.** The English judiciary, unlike the federal courts, gives advisory opinions to the other departments of government (p. 115).
- **What these teach:**
  - Bickel's own example shows a sovereign parliament behaving with restraint without judicial review, and suggests review can weaken a legislature's care for principle. For Israel the question is whether review has had that effect on the Knesset.
  - With a ceremonial president, the Court's symbolic role may be less needed, or more contested.

**What carries over regardless of structure**

- The tension between principle and expediency, and the Lincolnian answer to it (pp. 64–72).
- Upholding a law as an intervention in its own right (pp. 129–131).
- The passive virtues and the colloquy (Ch. 4).
- Delegation as a demand that the legislature decide fundamental questions itself (pp. 159–161).
- The "obligation to succeed" (p. 239).
- These describe a court facing elected institutions in general. Bickel transfers best on the Court's techniques and least on the structure of the difficulty.

### 1. Judicial review that "cannot be found" in the text

- **Bickel:** U.S. judicial review is not in the constitutional text; its justification must be argued "as a choice in our own time" (pp. 1, 12, 16).
- **Israel:**
  - In CA 6821/93 *United Mizrahi Bank v. Migdal Cooperative Village* (9 November 1995), Barak's lead opinion held that the Knesset adopts Basic Laws as a constituent authority, which gives them supra-legislative status.
  - Neither of the 1992 Basic Laws expressly grants judicial review. The limitation clause (section 8 of Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty) supplied the test, and the power to strike down was inferred from legal tradition.
  - The Harari Decision (13 June 1950) had left the constitution to be written chapter by chapter as Basic Laws.
- **What it teaches:** test both founding moments against Bickel's Chapter 1. Which arguments are textual, which historical, which functional? And does the absence of a single ratified document strengthen or weaken the counter-majoritarian difficulty?

### 2. The counter-majoritarian difficulty without Bickel's "troika"

- **Bickel:** representation is spread across "a troika of institutions" (p. 193). The separately elected executive "tends to cure inequities of over- and underrepresentation" (p. 18). See the first test above.
- **Israel:** a unicameral Knesset; a government that holds office with its confidence; no federalism; a ceremonial president.
- **What it teaches:** fewer elected veto points might make the Court a more necessary check, or a more exposed one.

### 3. Justiciability: passive virtues versus broad justiciability

- **Bickel:** standing, ripeness and the political question doctrine are the Court's essential tools. The political question doctrine rests on four elements: strangeness, momentousness, the anxiety that judgment should but will not be ignored, and self-doubt (p. 184).
- **Israel:**
  - HCJ 910/86 *Ressler v. Minister of Defence* (12 June 1988) took a liberal approach to standing, admitting public petitioners.
  - It distinguished normative from institutional justiciability (*shfitut*). The Versa headnote reads: "Two forms of justiciability should be distinguished - normative and institutional justiciability." Another headnote says: "All activities, including those of a political nature or matters of policy, are governed by legal norms of one kind or another."
  - The view is often summed up as "melo kol ha'aretz mishpat" ("the whole earth is full of law") and attributed to Barak. I could not find the phrase in the *Ressler* opinion, so treat that attribution as unconfirmed.
- **A Bickelian counter-example:** HCJ 3429/11 *Alumni Association of the Arab Orthodox School in Haifa v. Minister of Finance* (15 January 2012) dismissed the challenge to the "Nakba Law" as unripe for "absence of a concrete factual background." That reads like *Poe* or *Times Film*.
- **What it teaches:** whether Israel rejected Bickel's passive virtues or kept some of them under other names.

### 4. Upholding a law is not neutral

- **Bickel:** declaring a statute "not intolerable" is "a significant intervention in the political process, different in degree only from the sort of intervention marked by a declaration of unconstitutionality" (p. 129). On Jackson's warning: "The principle then lies about like a loaded weapon" (p. 131). His name for the mistake is "Plessy v. Ferguson's Error" (p. 197). If the political institutions insist on a course that cannot be squared with principle, "it is no part of the function of the Court to bless it, however double-negatively" (p. 188).
- **Israel:**
  - In HCJ 5555/18 *Hassoun v. Knesset* (8 July 2021), an 11-justice panel, voting 10–1, rejected all 15 petitions against Basic Law: Israel as the Nation-State of the Jewish People.
  - President Hayut did not decide whether the Court may review Basic Laws at all. Because she found the law did not negate Israel's character as Jewish and democratic, she did not need to.
- **What it teaches:** the Court upheld the law on the merits while leaving its own power to review Basic Laws undecided. That mixes Bickel's legitimation with his third option, doing neither, on the question of jurisdiction. It also raises what exactly the upholding legitimated.

### 5. Colloquy devices, Israeli style

- **Delegation.**
  - Bickel: "The more fundamental the issue, the nearer it is to principle, the more important it is that it be decided in the first instance by the legislature" (p. 161).
  - The Israeli doctrine of primary arrangements (*hesderim rishoniyim*) makes the same demand. In HCJ 3267/97 and 715/98 *Rubinstein v. Minister of Defense* (9 December 1998), the Court held that blanket draft deferrals for yeshiva students were "a primary arrangement that must be addressed through primary legislation, not administrative regulations." It delayed the invalidity by 12 months to give the Knesset time to act.
  - Compare *Kent v. Dulles*: "This was remanding to Congress for a second look" (p. 165).
- **Warning instead of striking.**
  - HCJ 5969/20 *Shafir v. Knesset* (23 May 2021, 6–3) found misuse of constituent power in temporary budget amendments to the Basic Laws. Instead of annulling them, it issued a notice of invalidation (*hatra'at batlut*) for the future.
  - That is close kin to Bickel's judgment whose "end is to pose a question, not to impose an answer" (p. 161).
- **What it teaches:** how far Israeli practice already uses what Bickel calls "the mediating way between legitimation and denial of legislative power" (p. 71). The 12-month delay in *Rubinstein* and the warning in *Shafir* are both ways of giving the legislature time to reply.

### 6. Review of the constitution's own amendments

- **Bickel:** never discusses judicial review of constitutional amendments; a search of the book confirms this. His materials cut both ways:
  - "Having been checked, should the people persist; having been educated, should the people insist, must they not win over every fundamental principle save one—which is the principle that they must win?" (p. 28). And: "First, on the supreme occasion, when the system is forced to find ultimate self-consistency, the principle of self-rule must prevail" (p. 261).
  - Against that: "The familiarity of amendment will breed a species of contempt and incapacitate the document for symbolic service" (p. 105).
- **Israel:**
  - In HCJ 5658/23 *Movement for Quality Government v. Knesset* (1 January 2024), 12 of 15 justices held the Court may review Basic Laws in extreme cases where the Knesset exceeded its constituent authority.
  - 8 of 15 voided Amendment No. 3 to Basic Law: The Judiciary, which had barred reasonableness review of decisions by the government, the Prime Minister and ministers.
  - Most Basic Law provisions can be amended by an ordinary majority (see the first test above).
- **What it teaches:** this is the sharpest difference between the systems. Bickel's American "extreme minority veto" (p. 21) is absent, and his p. 28 question reads differently where the majority can rewrite the constitution at will.

### 7. Reasonableness (*svirut*) versus Thayer's rule

- **Bickel:**
  - Thayer's rule is deferential rationality review of *legislation*. The relevant line is "between rationality and will" (p. 41).
  - Bickel treats review of *officials* as easier to justify. They "stand in closer relation to the electoral process than does the Court, but there is not between them and the Court the gulf that exists between the latter and the truly representative institutions, such as the legislature or elected executives" (p. 202).
  - And "the Court accords to the responsible political institutions a degree of deference that it does not owe to officials" (p. 214).
- **Israel:** HCJ 389/80 *Dapei Zahav v. Broadcasting Authority* (1980) recognized unreasonableness as an independent ground for invalidating an administrative decision. The January 2024 judgment cites it as the reference point. The 2023 amendment tried to remove this review for decisions of the government, the Prime Minister and ministers.
- **What it teaches:** a parliamentary wrinkle in Bickel's categories. His "officials" are appointed administrators; Israeli ministers are elected politicians answerable to the Knesset. On his own terms, review of cabinet decisions sits closer to review of "the responsible political institutions" than to review of officials. Review of civil servants stays on the easier side of his line.

### 8. Decree versus "political rule," and dependence on the executive

- **Bickel:**
  - Lincoln said of *Dred Scott*, "We offer no *resistance* to it," yet "we nevertheless do oppose that decision as a political rule" (p. 260).
  - The Court is "a court of last resort presumptively only" (p. 258).
  - "In an enforcement crisis of any real proportions, the judiciary is wholly dependent upon the Executive" (p. 252).
  - "For enforcement is a crisis of the system, not its norm" (p. 266).
- **Israel:** in July and August 2023, Prime Minister Netanyahu repeatedly declined to say directly whether he would abide by a ruling striking down the reasonableness amendment. He called the prospect "uncharted territory" and said, "I hope that they don't strike it down."
- **What it teaches:**
  - Which Israeli responses would count as Lincoln-style lawful resistance to a principle, and which would cross into Bickel's forbidden category of defying decrees.
  - In a parliamentary system the Executive Bickel depends on is the coalition itself (see the first test above).

### 9. Formal override versus informal reprise

- **Bickel:** the possibility of an answer from Congress makes some of the Court's errors bearable. Nonconstitutional decisions draw replies: "there are dozens of instances, in the federal context, of congressional reversal of the Court" (p. 206). In the Commerce Clause cases the Court's failures "have been rendered tolerable by the possibility of congressional revision" (p. 231). A constitutional judgment itself can be answered only politically, over time.
- **Israel:**
  - Section 8 of Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation, as re-enacted on 9 March 1994, lets a law passed by a majority of Knesset members take effect "notwithstanding the provisions of this Basic Law," for up to four years.
  - It was added after the High Court's 1993 Mitral (Meatrael) decision on the government's ban on non-kosher meat imports. That decision is commonly cited as HCJ 3872/93; I could not confirm the number.
  - The Knesset then passed the ban, and nine justices rejected petitions against it in HCJ 4676/94 *Meatrael v. Knesset*.
  - Proposals for a general override were part of the 2023 overhaul.
- **What it teaches:** whether a formal override institutionalizes Lincoln's "political rule," or replaces the slow colloquy Bickel valued with a one-step veto.

### 10. Appointments, continuity and the symbolic function

- **Bickel:** "To the extent that they are instruments of decisive change, Justices are time bombs, not warheads that explode on impact" (p. 31). In America, "it has in large part been left to the Supreme Court to concretize the symbol of the Constitution" (p. 31).
- **Israel (check current status):**
  - The March 2025 amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary passed 67–1. It replaces the Israel Bar Association's two seats on the Judicial Selection Committee with political appointees, one from the coalition and one from the opposition. It also requires five votes instead of seven for Supreme Court appointments.
  - It takes effect when the next Knesset begins its term.
  - All 11 serving justices heard petitions against it on 21 June 2026. As of 6 September 2026 the Court had not ruled, and I found no later report of a ruling.
  - The 26th Knesset is sworn in on 10 November 2026, which is when the amendment would take effect. Check whether the Court has ruled and whether the amendment is now in force.
- **What it teaches:** how appointment rules change the Court's pace of doctrinal change and its claim to embody continuity. A parliamentary coalition that controls appointments directly can shorten Bickel's "time bomb" fuse.

### 11. Whose acceptance counts?

- **Bickel:** principles must be able to win what Justice Frankfurter called "widespread acceptance" (pp. 238–239). Only national returns count: "surely the relevant returns would be those from the nation as a whole, not from a white majority in a given region. Fragmented returns cannot count, any more than early ones" (p. 250). The Court "labors under the obligation to succeed" (p. 239).
- **What it teaches:** in a deeply divided polity, what counts as returns "from the nation as a whole." Also whether the Israeli Court can meet Bickel's obligation to succeed when the divisions are not regional, as the Southern resistance was, but run through the whole society.
